Rotator Cuff Repair in Overhead Athletes: Measurable Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the least forgiving return-to-sport problems in MSK practice. The tissue has to heal, the shoulder has to tolerate repeated high-velocity loading, and the athlete usually wants to be throwing, serving or swimming well before either is guaranteed. Most clinics still make the call with a mixture of surgeon protocol, a manual muscle test and a feeling that the shoulder looks ready.

This article sets out what a defensible return-to-sport decision looks like after cuff repair, which prognostic factors deserve your attention, and where the evidence is thinner than the confidence of many protocols suggests.

What the evidence base can and cannot tell you

Be clear about the limits first. Shoulder return-to-sport research is far smaller than the ACL literature, and high-quality trials specific to cuff repair in overhead athletes are scarce. The most useful guidance comes from consensus work and from adjacent shoulder conditions, which we can use for structure but should not treat as cuff-specific proof.

The 2022 Bern consensus statement on shoulder injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport is the best anchor available. It frames return to sport as a continuum rather than a single date, and it supports using clinical and functional criteria to guide progression across rehabilitation stages (Schwank et al., 2022). That is a consensus position, not a validated cut-off set, and it should be read that way.

Evidence from other shoulder conditions shows the same gap. A systematic review of nonsurgical management of SLAP tears found that return-to-play outcomes were reported, but the criteria used to decide readiness were heterogeneous across studies (Steinmetz et al., 2022). A review of operative and nonoperative care for anterior shoulder instability reaches a similar place: return-to-sport decisions rest on a combination of clinical and functional factors, with limited agreement on exact thresholds (Herman et al., 2024). If you are waiting for a published cuff-repair threshold to apply, you will be waiting a long time.

Practical takeaway: treat any single published number with suspicion, and build your criteria from measurable, comparable data you collect consistently on every patient.

Criteria, not dates

Surgeon protocols set the protective constraints after repair, and those gates must be respected. Within them, the progression decision should be driven by what the patient can demonstrate, not by the week count alone.

The argument for criteria-based progression is best developed outside the shoulder. The multifactorial hamstring algorithm of Mendiguchia and colleagues progresses athletes through stages by meeting explicit functional criteria rather than by elapsed time (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). It is a different joint and a different tissue, so the specific tests do not transfer. The principle does: define what must be true before the next load is added, and record whether it is true.

For a cuff repair, that means each stage has written, measurable entry criteria. A patient who has reached week 16 but cannot meet them has not progressed, and a patient who meets them within the surgeon's constraints should not be held back by habit. Variation between clinicians in these calls is the thing you are trying to remove.

  • Write the criteria for each stage before the patient reaches it.
  • Record the result of each criterion at every review, not only when you suspect a problem.
  • Where a criterion is not met, record which one and what you changed in response.

What to measure and how to express it

A useful battery for an overhead athlete covers range, endurance, strength and power, alongside what the patient tells you. None of it needs expensive hardware.

  1. Range of movement. Inclinometer measures of elevation and rotation, using whichever inclinometer tool your clinic already has. The point is repeatable measurement at each review, not a particular device.
  2. Strength. Handheld dynamometry for external rotation, internal rotation and abduction, expressed as a percentage of the normative expectation for age, sex and weight from peer-reviewed data. As rehabilitation moves into loaded work, gym-based lifts such as a press or row can be compared against Strengthlevel normative data in the same way.
  3. Endurance. Timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols. Overhead sport is repetitive, so a shoulder that produces one good effort and fatigues by the tenth is not ready for a training session.
  4. Power. Throws are the natural fit for the upper limb. A seated medicine ball throw measured with a tape measure gives a distance you can compare against expected performance and repeat at every review.

Expressing each result as a percentage of expected score gives you a common language across tests. It also avoids a trap that catches many shoulder protocols: comparing the operated side with the other side. In an overhead athlete the dominant arm is often adapted by years of throwing, so the uninvolved side is a poor reference. A normative comparison matched to the patient avoids that problem.

Because the question is how close to expected performance the athlete is, you can set stage criteria as a percentage of expected score for each domain and track movement towards them. Where a domain falls below the normative threshold, that is your prompt to select and dose exercise for that specific deficit, with defined sets, reps, load, rest and weekly volume.

Prognostic factors worth recording

Several factors shape the likelihood and speed of return, and the honest position is that some are better supported than others.

Tear characteristics, repair integrity and patient age influence outcome in the surgical literature, and the surgeon holds most of that information. Make sure it reaches you in the referral. It determines how conservative your early criteria need to be, and it is the one part of the picture you cannot measure in clinic.

Psychological factors are better supported across sport injury generally. A systematic review of psychological factors associated with returning to sport found that confidence, fear of re-injury and motivation were consistently linked to whether athletes return and at what level (Ardern et al., 2013). That review is not specific to the shoulder, and Benchmark does not include a psychological-readiness instrument. What you can do is take the finding seriously in conversation and use the patient-specific data you do have. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) lets the athlete name the activities that matter, such as serving, overhead lifting or a full throwing session, and rate each from 0 to 10. A patient whose physical results are strong but whose PSFS items are lagging is giving you information that strength numbers will not.

The three-dimensional pain score, the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration, adds to this. A single pain intensity rating hides a lot. An athlete with low intensity but high frequency during throwing is in a different position from one with occasional sharp pain, and the mean of the three dimensions tracks change more faithfully over a review period. Interpreting both measures against the minimal clinically important difference and expected rate of change stops you from calling a two-point wobble progress or a plateau.

Activity level matters too. Checking it against NHS guidelines flags patients who are well below recommended levels, which is common after months of reduced loading and affects both general recovery and how much capacity you are building from.

Making the final return-to-sport decision

Return to sport is not one decision. It is usually graded: non-throwing training, graded throwing or serving, practice, then competition. The Bern consensus supports this staged view of return (Schwank et al., 2022), and nothing in the wider shoulder literature argues for a single pass-or-fail test.

A defensible final decision pulls together four things on the same page: the surgeon's clearance, the percentage-of-expected results across range, strength, endurance and power, the PSFS and pain trends against MCID, and the athlete's own account of readiness. If these disagree, the disagreement is the finding. A strong dynamometer result with a lagging PSFS throwing item means you keep working on that item before clearing the athlete, not that the number wins.

Longitudinal tracking earns its keep here. Seeing objective results and patient-reported scores plotted together over the course of rehabilitation shows whether the trajectory is steady or has stalled, and it gives the athlete, coach and surgeon the same record to look at. That is easier to defend than a note saying the shoulder felt good.

A practical next step

If your current cuff pathway progresses mainly by week count and manual testing, start small. Pick two or three criteria for your next post-repair athlete, such as handheld dynamometry for rotation, a seated medicine ball throw distance and the PSFS, and record them at every review. After five or six patients you will have your own picture of what a typical trajectory looks like, and where your athletes stall.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a validated set of return-to-sport criteria after rotator cuff repair?

Not one that is widely accepted. Consensus guidance supports staged, criteria-based return, but exact thresholds are not agreed. Clinics should set measurable criteria, record them consistently and review them against their own data.

Why not compare the operated shoulder with the other side?

In overhead athletes the dominant arm is often adapted by years of repetitive loading, so the other side is an unreliable reference. Comparing results with a normative expectation for age, sex and weight avoids that problem.

Which tests suit a clinic without specialist equipment?

Inclinometer-based range of movement, handheld dynamometry for rotation and abduction, timed holds or repetition tests for endurance, and a seated medicine ball throw measured with a tape measure for power.

How do patient-reported measures fit into the decision?

The PSFS captures the specific overhead tasks the athlete needs to return to, and the three-dimensional pain score tracks frequency, intensity and duration. Read against MCID, they show whether physical gains are translating into function.
