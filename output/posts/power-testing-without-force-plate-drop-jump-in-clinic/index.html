Power Testing Without a Force Plate: What the Drop Jump Can Tell You in Clinic

Strength gets measured. Range of movement gets measured. Power, more often than not, gets guessed. A patient squats well, reports feeling strong, and the clinician makes a judgement call about whether they're ready to jump, cut, or throw again. That judgement call is where a lot of rehab pathways quietly lose their rigour, because power is the quality most closely tied to sport-specific demand and the one least likely to be tested with anything objective.

The usual excuse is equipment. Force plates and jump mats are expensive, need calibration, and aren't practical in a 30-minute slot in a general MSK clinic. But the drop jump doesn't require any of that. With a smartphone app and a tape measure you can extract genuinely useful power data, and used well, it closes one of the biggest gaps in conservative and post-surgical rehab.

Why power testing gets skipped

Most rehab pathways are built around strength and range of movement because those are the easiest things to measure with equipment already sitting in the treatment room. Reviews of rehabilitation protocols after major knee and shoulder procedures consistently show wide variation in what gets tracked and when, with strength and range of movement dominating the outcome measures used across studies and power rarely appearing at all (Dávila et al., 2019). The same pattern shows up in rotator cuff repair rehabilitation, where protocol reviews highlight inconsistent criteria for progressing patients through phases, particularly around when loading intensity should increase (Bandara et al., 2021).

That gap matters because power is not just "strength done quickly". It reflects the neuromuscular system's ability to produce force rapidly and absorb load repeatedly, which is exactly what's demanded in change of direction, landing, and overhead deceleration. A patient can pass a manual muscle test and a goniometer check and still be nowhere near ready for that demand. Testing power doesn't need to be complicated to close that gap, but it does need to happen.

What the drop jump actually tells you

A drop jump asks a patient to step off a low box, land, and immediately rebound into a maximal vertical jump. Two numbers come out of it that matter clinically: how long the patient spent on the ground during the transition (ground contact time) and how high they jumped afterwards. Divide jump height by contact time and you get the reactive strength index, a simple ratio that describes how efficiently a patient converts landing force into upward propulsion.

You don't need a force plate to get either number. Ground contact time can be timed with a basic smartphone app such as MyJump2, and jump height is easy enough to estimate from video or a simple reach-and-mark test with a tape measure against a wall. The output is less precise than laboratory-grade equipment, but it's precise enough to track meaningful change in an individual patient over time, which is the point of testing in clinic rather than in a performance lab.

What you're looking for is not a single impressive number. It's a pattern: does reactive strength index improve session to session as loading increases, does it hold steady across repeated jumps or drop off after two or three (a fatigue signal worth flagging), and how does the result compare to what's expected for the patient's age, sex, and sport. A raw score without that context tells you very little. A score expressed as a percentage of what's normal for that patient tells you whether they're behind, on track, or ready to progress.

Where power testing earns its place in the rehab timeline

Power testing isn't a day-one tool. Early after ACL reconstruction or meniscus repair, the priority is protecting the graft or repair while re-establishing range of movement and basic quadriceps activation, and open kinetic chain loading in those first weeks needs to be sequenced carefully rather than avoided outright (Fontanier et al., 2025). Reviews of meniscus repair rehabilitation similarly point to wide inconsistency in when patients are cleared to load and progress, with return-to-sport timelines varying substantially between protocols that use no shared testing framework (Harput et al., 2020).

Power testing becomes useful once a patient has cleared the basic strength and range thresholds and the clinical question shifts from "is it safe to load" to "is it ready for sport-specific demand". That's where a drop jump earns its place; not as a pass/fail gate on day one, but as a repeatable checkpoint through the later stages of a pathway. The same logic applies in the shoulder, where a rotator cuff repair or overhead athlete's return to throwing depends on the ability to absorb and redirect force rapidly through the kinetic chain, not just static strength in isolation.

The broader lesson from the rehabilitation literature is that protocols vary enormously when there's no shared measurement framework to anchor decisions to. Early post-operative pathways for major joint procedures show the same issue: trials on early physiotherapy after total knee arthroplasty report meaningful gains in pain and range of movement, but the criteria used to decide when a patient moves between phases differ from study to study (Aftab et al., 2025). Power testing won't fix that on its own, but it adds one more objective data point that removes some of the guesswork from a decision that's often currently made on how a patient "looks" rather than what they can measure.

Building it into a normal clinic slot

The practical barrier clinicians raise isn't whether the drop jump is useful, it's whether it fits into a session that's already tight. It does, if you treat it as part of the same low-tech testing kit you're likely already using for strength and range. A box or step, a tape measure, and a phone with a jump-timing app take under five minutes to set up and run three trials.

  • Use a box height the patient can manage safely, typically 20-30cm for most rehab populations
  • Run three trials and take the best or most consistent result, not the average
  • Record ground contact time and jump height, then calculate reactive strength index
  • Compare the result against the patient's expected score for age, sex, and activity level, not a generic published cutoff
  • Repeat at fixed intervals through later-stage rehab, not just once at discharge

The value isn't the single test. It's the trend line. A patient whose reactive strength index climbs steadily across four sessions as their strength testing also improves gives you a defensible reason to progress their loading. One whose numbers plateau or drop, despite feeling "fine" subjectively, gives you a reason to slow down, and a much better conversation to have with the patient than "it doesn't look ready yet".

Where the drop jump fits, and where it doesn't

A drop jump measures one thing well: reactive power under a landing-and-rebound demand. It doesn't tell you about strength in isolation, it doesn't tell you about range of movement, and it isn't a substitute for the patient-reported picture of how their knee, shoulder, or back actually feels day to day. Digital and remote-monitoring approaches to musculoskeletal rehab have shown that structured, technology-enabled tracking can match conventional in-person care on pain and function outcomes when it's built around consistent measurement rather than replacing clinical judgement (Pak et al., 2023). The drop jump works the same way: it's one input into a bigger decision, not a stand-alone verdict.

Used alongside strength testing, range of movement, and the patient's own functional scale, it gives you a rounded, objective picture instead of a subjective one. That's the whole point of testing power in the first place: not to replace clinical reasoning, but to give it something firmer to stand on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a force plate to test power in clinic?

No. A smartphone jump-timing app and a tape measure give you enough data to calculate jump height and ground contact time, which is all you need for a usable reactive strength index in a clinical setting.

When in a rehab pathway should power testing start?

Once a patient has cleared basic strength and range of movement thresholds and can land and load safely. It's a later-stage progression tool, not a day-one screening test.

What does the drop jump actually measure?

It measures reactive strength: how efficiently a patient converts landing force into a subsequent jump, calculated as jump height divided by ground contact time.

Is a single drop jump score useful on its own?

Not much. The value comes from tracking the trend over repeated sessions and comparing the result to what's expected for that patient's age, sex, and activity level, rather than judging one number in isolation.
