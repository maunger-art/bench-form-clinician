Rotator Cuff Repair in Overhead Athletes: Measurable Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the harder return-to-sport decisions in musculoskeletal practice. The tissue needs protection for longer than most athletes will tolerate. The demands of throwing, serving or swimming are high and repetitive. The athlete is usually pushing for a date.

Most clinicians will have cleared an athlete on the basis of full range, a pain-free examination and a feeling that the shoulder looks strong. That is a defensible clinical impression, but it is hard to reproduce between clinicians and harder to explain to a surgeon, a coach or the athlete. This article sets out which prognostic factors deserve attention and how to build return-to-sport criteria that are measurable and comparable.

A note on the evidence. The approved literature for this article contains no rotator cuff repair trials. The shoulder sources cited here are consensus and review level, and they cover shoulder injury more broadly. Where a claim goes beyond them, we say so rather than stretch a citation.

Why return-to-sport decisions after cuff repair go wrong

The first problem is that the calendar tends to do the work that data should do. Surgical protocols give sensible protection windows, and those windows matter for tendon healing. But once the protection phase ends, many athletes are progressed by elapsed weeks and by whether the shoulder feels acceptable, not by what it can do.

The 2022 Bern Consensus Statement on shoulder injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport frames return to sport as a continuum of rehabilitation, return to training and return to competition, and it supports decisions based on the athlete's status against defined criteria (Schwank et al., 2022). That structure fits cuff repair well, even though the statement is not specific to it. The practical implication is that each stage needs an exit test, not just an end date.

The same logic has been formalised in other regions. A criteria-based progressive algorithm for hamstring injury treatment moves athletes through stages when they meet defined criteria, not when a period has elapsed (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). The tissue and the demands are different, but the principle transfers: if progression depends on a measurement, two clinicians looking at the same athlete should reach the same decision.

Prognostic factors worth recording at baseline

Some factors that shape the outlook after cuff repair are fixed by the time the athlete reaches you. Others you can influence. Recording both at the outset gives you a reference point and keeps the conversation with the athlete honest.

Factors that are generally considered when estimating the outlook, and that come from the surgical record, not from the approved reference pack, include:

  • Tear size and the number of tendons involved
  • Tissue quality at surgery, as described in the operative note
  • Age and sport level
  • Whether the tear was acute or developed over time
  • Any concurrent pathology treated at the same operation

Ask the surgeon for these details and put them in the record. They will not change your testing, but they should change how cautiously you read the results. An athlete who reaches a given score with a large repair deserves a more conservative plan than one with a small, well-vascularised repair.

The modifiable factors are the ones you can measure repeatedly: shoulder strength relative to what is expected for the athlete, range of movement, throwing power, and the patient's own report of function and pain. These are the basis for the criteria that follow.

Building measurable criteria with basic equipment

Benchmark PS is technology-agnostic. Every test below can be done with low-cost tools, and the output is expressed against the score you would expect for that athlete, not against a threshold borrowed from another joint.

Strength as a percentage of expected

Handheld dynamometry is the practical route for shoulder strength in clinic. Benchmark compares handheld dynamometer data with peer-reviewed normative values and reports the result as a percentage of normative expectation for the athlete's age, sex and weight. Where the athlete is training in the gym, for example pressing or rowing, gym-based lifts can be compared against Strengthlevel.com normative data in the same way.

This changes how you think about the target. In a repaired shoulder, the uninvolved side is not necessarily a reliable reference, particularly in a throwing athlete whose dominant arm may be adapted. Comparing the athlete with an age-, sex- and weight-matched expectation gives you a stable number that does not depend on the other arm. Benchmark does not calculate a left-to-right comparison or apply a fixed symmetry threshold, and we would not suggest building cuff criteria on one.

For overhead athletes, rotation strength is the obvious priority. We have covered the external to internal rotation relationship in a separate article, and the same testing approach applies here.

Range of movement

Range of movement can be recorded with any inclinometer tool and entered into the platform. Record elevation and rotation at each review using the same position and the same device. The value is in the trend. A plateau in range at a given visit is information about the next two weeks of rehabilitation, and it is only visible if the measurement was consistent.

Endurance

Overhead sport is repetitive, so a shoulder that produces a good single effort and then fades is not ready. Use timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols suited to the athlete's sport and record the result at each stage. Repeat the same protocol every time, so that the numbers can be compared.

Power

Throwing and overhead athletes need to produce force quickly. A seated or standing medicine ball throw, measured with a tape measure, is a low-cost way to track upper-limb power. Distance is the output, and it can be compared across visits and against the athlete's expected value. If you have a force plate or jump mat, they can be used, but the tape measure is enough to generate decision-grade data.

Patient-reported function and pain

Benchmark uses two patient-reported measures. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale lets the athlete name the activities they cannot yet do, such as a full-effort serve or a long throw, and score them from 0 to 10. The three-dimensional pain score takes the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration. MCID and expected rate of change are provided alongside the scores, so you can judge whether a movement of two points is meaningful or noise.

Putting the criteria into stages

A staged framework might look like this. The thresholds are for you and the surgical team to set. The point is that each one is a measurable number, not a date.

  1. Protection to loading. Range of movement at the level agreed with the surgeon, pain scores trending down, and the athlete able to complete the prescribed loading without a flare-up in the next 24 hours.
  2. Loading to return to training. Handheld dynamometry for key shoulder actions at an agreed percentage of expected, endurance protocol completed at the target repetitions or hold time, and PSFS items moving by more than the MCID.
  3. Return to training to return to sport. Strength at or near expected for age, sex and weight, medicine ball throw distance at the agreed level, and the athlete's PSFS activities close to their pre-injury score.

Where the athlete falls below a normative threshold on a test, Benchmark suggests exercises and training parameters, including sets, reps, load, rest, frequency and weekly volume per muscle group, with progressions and regressions. That keeps the rehabilitation plan tied to the test result.

It is also worth looking at how other shoulder problems are handled in the literature. Reviews of return to play after nonsurgical management of SLAP tears (Steinmetz et al., 2022) and of rehabilitation after operative and nonoperative treatment of anterior shoulder instability (Herman et al., 2024) show how return to sport is reported in this joint. They are not cuff repair data, and their findings should not be applied directly to your repair patients. They are useful for seeing how the field defines and reports return.

The athlete's side of the decision

Numbers do not make the decision alone. A systematic review of psychological factors associated with returning to sport after injury found that psychological factors are linked to whether athletes return and at what level (Ardern et al., 2013). That review is not specific to the shoulder, but it supports something most clinicians already see: an athlete with good strength who does not trust the arm may not return, and one who is desperate to compete may hide symptoms.

Benchmark does not measure psychological readiness, and we do not suggest it as a feature. What the platform does give you is a record of objective progress that you can share with the athlete. Seeing a rising percentage of expected strength and a falling pain score is often a more persuasive basis for a conversation about confidence than reassurance alone. If the numbers are met and the athlete still hesitates, that is a clinical conversation to have, and your judgement leads it.

Practical points to take from this:

  • Record surgical and tissue-quality details at baseline so that you can interpret the results with context.
  • Test the same actions with the same tools at each review.
  • Express strength as a percentage of expected for the athlete's age, sex and weight, not as a comparison with the other arm.
  • Use endurance and a simple throw to test the demands of the sport, not just a single maximal effort.
  • Agree the criteria with the surgeon before the athlete starts asking about dates.

Frequently Asked Questions

What objective measures can guide return to sport after rotator cuff repair?

Shoulder strength measured with a handheld dynamometer and expressed as a percentage of expected for age, sex and weight, range of movement from an inclinometer, endurance holds or repetition tests, medicine ball throw distance, and the PSFS and three-dimensional pain score. The surgical team should agree the thresholds.

Why not compare the repaired shoulder with the other side?

In overhead athletes the dominant arm is often adapted, so the other side may not be a fair reference. Benchmark compares results with normative expectation for age, sex and weight and does not calculate limb symmetry.

Does Benchmark PS assess psychological readiness for return to sport?

No. Psychological factors are associated with return to sport after injury, but Benchmark only collects the PSFS and the three-dimensional pain score as patient-reported measures. Clinicians still need to discuss confidence with the athlete directly.
