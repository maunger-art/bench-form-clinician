Rotator Cuff Repair in Overhead Athletes: Measurable Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the harder return-to-sport decisions in MSK practice. The tissue needs protecting, the sport demands repeated high-velocity loading, and the surgeon's protocol often gives you a calendar rather than a set of standards. Most clinicians have cleared a thrower or swimmer because the timeline said so and the shoulder felt reasonable on manual testing. This article sets out what a criteria-based approach looks like, which prognostic factors deserve your attention, and how to turn each into a number you can record and revisit.

Why calendar-led clearance falls short

A repair protocol gives you phases, and phases are sensible for protecting healing tissue early on. The problem comes later. Two athletes at the same post-operative stage can have very different capacity, and the calendar cannot tell you which one you are looking at. The Bern consensus statement on shoulder injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport makes the point that return to sport should be a structured, criteria-informed process that considers the athlete's sport demands and not a single date or a single test (Schwank et al., 2022).

Other areas of sports rehabilitation have moved the same way. The criteria-based progressive algorithm developed for hamstring injury replaces time-driven progression with explicit criteria that must be met before the athlete moves to the next stage (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). The tissue and the sport are different, but the logic transfers directly: define the criteria, measure them, and progress when they are met.

A practical takeaway: before the athlete is in your clinic, agree with the surgeon which restrictions are fixed protection limits and which are open to progression on objective criteria. Write both down at the first appointment so the athlete knows what is being measured and why.

Prognostic factors worth recording at baseline

Prognosis after cuff repair is shaped by factors you can partly see on day one. Tear size and tissue quality are known from the operative report. Age, the athlete's sport and position, and whether the dominant arm is involved all change the realistic target. A collegiate pitcher and a recreational club swimmer with the same repair do not share the same return-to-sport question.

Request the operative note and record the following in the file:

  • Tear size, tendons involved and repair type
  • Any associated pathology, such as labral or instability findings
  • Sport, position and the specific overhead demand (throwing, serving, swimming stroke, overhead lifting)
  • Pre-injury performance level and the level the athlete intends to return to
  • Baseline PSFS items, with the athlete naming their own functional limits

This is where the evidence base is thinner than we would like. The approved literature I can draw on here covers shoulder return to sport more broadly, including nonoperative SLAP tears (Steinmetz et al., 2022) and anterior shoulder instability (Herman et al., 2024), and not cuff repair in isolation. Treat those papers as a guide to how shoulder return-to-play questions are framed, not as cuff-specific prognostic data. Where a claim about cuff-specific outcomes is not supported by what we have here, it is left uncited on purpose.

Range of movement: a gate, not a target

Range of movement is usually the first measurable criterion because it limits everything that follows. For overhead athletes, record elevation and rotation with an inclinometer at a consistent position each time. Benchmark accepts data from any inclinometer tool, so the method matters less than using the same one, in the same position, every session.

Two practical points. First, rotation range in a thrower's dominant shoulder differs from the general population, so a population norm is not always the right comparator and the athlete's pre-injury range, where known, carries weight. Second, range alone does not clear an athlete. Treat it as a gate that must be passed before higher-load testing begins, and record the reading so the trend is visible across weeks.

Strength and endurance: expressing capacity against expectation

Strength is where subjective assessment is weakest. A manual muscle test graded as 'good' cannot distinguish between an athlete who is close to expected capacity and one who is well below it. Handheld dynamometry gives you a number, and Benchmark compares handheld dynamometer data against peer-reviewed normative values, expressing the result as a percentage of expected score for the athlete's age, sex and weight.

For a post-repair shoulder, test the movements that match the sport demand and keep the position standardised: same arm position, same lever arm, same instructions, same number of trials. Without that standardisation a change in the number may reflect a change in technique rather than a change in capacity.

Endurance is often missed, and overhead athletes rarely fail at one maximal effort. They fail on the fortieth throw or the last length. Timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols give you a measure of repeated-effort capacity. Record the time or repetitions achieved, repeat the same protocol at each review, and look at the trajectory.

A useful working approach:

  1. Test strength and endurance at fixed intervals using the same protocol each time
  2. Record each result as a percentage of expected score, not as a raw number alone
  3. Where the athlete falls below the normative threshold, use that finding to direct exercise selection, sets, reps, load and weekly volume
  4. Retest, and progress only when the criteria you set at the start are met

Power: testing the demand the sport actually makes

Overhead sport is a power activity, and a strength score does not tell you whether the athlete can express force quickly. Power testing for the upper limb does not need expensive equipment. A seated or standing medicine ball throw measured with a tape measure gives you distance, which you can record and repeat. Force plates can be used if you have them, but the cheaper options are perfectly adequate for clinical decisions.

Introduce throwing-based power testing only once range and strength criteria are met, and agree this with the surgical team. Record distance, ball mass and position, because the number is meaningless without them. As with strength, the aim is a repeatable measurement that shows direction of travel, not a single impressive result on a good day.

Patient-reported outcomes and the psychological dimension

Objective testing is half the picture. Benchmark uses two patient-reported measures: the PSFS, where the athlete names and rates their own functional limits on a 0 to 10 scale, and a three-dimensional pain score that averages frequency, intensity and duration. Both come with MCID and expected rate of change, which helps you separate a real change from measurement noise.

The PSFS is particularly useful for overhead athletes because the activities that matter are specific: a first serve, a full-effort throw, a butterfly stroke. Ask the athlete to name those activities and score them. If the objective results look acceptable but the PSFS items are not moving, that mismatch is a clinical finding that deserves a conversation.

Psychological factors also shape whether athletes return and at what level. A systematic review of the psychological factors associated with returning to sport after injury found that psychological responses are linked to return outcomes (Ardern et al., 2013). Benchmark does not measure psychological readiness, so this stays a matter for your clinical conversation with the athlete. The practical point is not to assume that meeting the physical criteria settles the question.

Putting the criteria together

A defensible return-to-sport decision rests on measurable criteria set in advance and reviewed against the data. For an overhead athlete after cuff repair, that means:

  • Range of movement recorded consistently and cleared as a gate
  • Strength expressed as a percentage of normative expectation
  • Endurance measured with a repeatable timed or repetition protocol
  • Power measured with a throw or jump that reflects sport demand
  • PSFS and pain scores tracked against MCID
  • A documented conversation with the surgeon and athlete about the return plan

This also makes your clinical reasoning easier to defend. If an athlete is cleared and then struggles, a record of criteria, results and decisions shows how the judgement was reached. If an athlete is held back, you can show them the specific measure that has not yet been met, which is more persuasive than a general instruction to wait. Consistent measurement across a caseload also shows where your own variation sits, which is the starting point for tighter, more consistent practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What criteria should an overhead athlete meet before returning to sport after rotator cuff repair?

A criteria-led approach combines range of movement, strength expressed as a percentage of normative expectation, endurance, sport-specific power, and patient-reported function tracked with the PSFS and a three-dimensional pain score. Criteria should be agreed with the surgeon and athlete at the start, and progression should depend on meeting them, not on the calendar alone.

Can I test shoulder strength after cuff repair without an isokinetic machine?

Yes. Handheld dynamometry using a standardised position and protocol gives a repeatable measure. Benchmark compares handheld dynamometer data against peer-reviewed normative values and expresses the result as a percentage of expected score for age, sex and weight.

How do I measure power in an overhead athlete without a force plate?

A medicine ball throw measured with a tape measure gives a repeatable distance score. Record the ball mass and the athlete's position each time so results are comparable across sessions. Introduce throwing-based testing only once earlier criteria are met and the surgical team agrees.
