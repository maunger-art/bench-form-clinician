Rotator Cuff Repair: Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors for Overhead Athletes

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the hardest return-to-sport decisions in MSK practice. The tissue needs protection, the sport demands repeated high-velocity loading, and the surgeon's protocol often gives you a calendar rather than a set of criteria. A calendar tells you when the tissue has had time to heal. It does not tell you whether the athlete can tolerate what the sport will ask of the shoulder.

This article sets out how to build return-to-sport criteria that are measurable, which prognostic factors should shape your expectations, and where the evidence is thinner than the confidence of some protocols suggests.

What the evidence base actually offers

It is worth being straightforward about the limits here. High-quality trials that compare return-to-sport criteria after rotator cuff repair in overhead athletes are scarce, and the athletic population is small compared with the general repair population. Most of what clinicians use is extrapolated from consensus guidance and from adjacent shoulder conditions.

The 2022 Bern Consensus Statement is the most useful framing document we have for shoulder rehabilitation across participation levels. It supports a staged, criteria-informed approach to rehabilitation and return to sport in athletes, rather than relying on elapsed time alone (Schwank et al., 2022). Reviews of other shoulder conditions point the same way. A systematic review of non-surgical SLAP tear management found that return to play was achievable for many athletes, but the way outcomes and readiness were defined varied between studies (Steinmetz et al., 2022). A review of operative and non-operative anterior shoulder instability reaches a similar position: rehabilitation progression and return-to-sport decisions benefit from objective checkpoints, and the quality of reporting on those checkpoints is inconsistent (Herman et al., 2024).

The practical reading is this. Where you cannot cite a rotator cuff specific threshold, do not invent one. Build criteria from measurable domains, compare them with a normative expectation, and document the reasoning.

Why time since surgery is a gate, not a criterion

Most surgeons set protective phases for tendon healing: sling use, passive range, then active range, then loading. Those phases are legitimate constraints. Respect them. But once the protective window has passed, weeks since surgery stops being useful for deciding whether the athlete is ready for throwing, serving, or swimming volume.

Two athletes at the same post-operative stage can differ enormously in strength, endurance and tolerance of load. Criteria-based progression has been described in detail for hamstring injury, where a multifactorial algorithm moves the athlete forward when defined criteria are met rather than when a date arrives (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). The shoulder equivalent is the same logic: the surgeon's timeline sets the earliest point at which you may test, and the test results decide whether you progress.

In practice that means writing the criteria down at the start of the loading phase, so that the athlete, the surgeon and the coach all know what has to be true before the next stage.

Building measurable criteria across four domains

For an overhead athlete, four physical domains give you a defensible picture. Each can be done with low-tech tools.

  1. Range of movement. Measure elevation, external rotation and internal rotation with any inclinometer you trust, and use the same tool and position at every visit. Overhead athletes often have an adapted rotation profile on the dominant side, so interpret the numbers against the demands of the sport rather than a generic textbook range.
  2. Strength. Handheld dynamometry for rotation and elevation, expressed as a percentage of the normative expectation for the athlete's age, sex and weight. Gym-based lifts, once the shoulder tolerates them, can be compared with normative lifting data in the same way. A percentage of expected score gives you something that stays meaningful when both shoulders are affected or when the pre-injury baseline is unknown.
  3. Endurance. Timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols in positions relevant to the sport. Throwing and serving are repeated efforts. A shoulder that produces an acceptable single maximal effort but fatigues in a set of repetitions is not ready for a full training session.
  4. Power. Medicine ball throws measured with a tape measure are cheap, repeatable and specific to overhead sport. Add a countermovement or single-leg jump with a smartphone app if lower limb and trunk contribution is part of the athlete's return plan, because the kinetic chain does much of the work in throwing.

Set the thresholds as a percentage of the normative expectation for each test, agree them with the surgeon where the repair is large, and record them. The point is to make the decision measurable and comparable from one visit to the next, and between clinicians in the same practice.

Prognostic factors that should change your expectations

Some factors influence how conservatively you should plan, and they should be discussed with the surgeon rather than guessed at. From clinical experience and operative reports, the following commonly shape the outlook, although they are not drawn from the references used in this article and the weight of each varies by study:

  • Tear size and tissue quality noted at surgery, including whether the repair was considered tension-free.
  • Whether the tear was acute and traumatic or a degenerative tear in a long-standing overhead athlete.
  • Age and training history, with older athletes generally needing longer to rebuild capacity.
  • Starting levels of strength and range at the point loading begins.
  • Sport demands, particularly the volume and velocity of overhead work, and whether the athlete is a thrower, a racket player or a swimmer.

Psychological factors deserve a place in this list too. A systematic review of psychological factors associated with returning to sport after injury found that confidence, motivation and fear of re-injury are linked to whether athletes return and to what level (Ardern et al., 2013). You do not need a dedicated questionnaire to act on this. Ask the athlete directly which tasks they avoid, and record those as patient-specific limitations using the Patient-Specific Functional Scale. A task the athlete rates as a 3 out of 10, such as an overhead serve or a full-effort throw, is a more useful target than a generic function score, and it can be tracked from visit to visit.

Using patient-reported measures alongside the physical tests

Strength and range tell you what the shoulder can do. The athlete's own account tells you what they believe it can do. Use both.

The PSFS lets the athlete name the activities they are limited in and rate them. The three-dimensional pain score, the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration, captures how often and how long pain is intruding, which is often more informative than a single intensity number for an athlete who is mostly pain-free at rest. Look at each score against the minimal clinically important difference and the expected rate of change, so that a two-point shift is interpreted properly rather than over-read.

When the physical tests are improving and the PSFS is not, the barrier may not be physical capacity. When the PSFS is improving and the strength percentage is lagging, the athlete may be about to do more than the shoulder can tolerate. Neither pattern is visible without both data sets plotted over time.

A practical checklist and next step

  1. Agree the surgeon's protective phases and the earliest date for each loading stage.
  2. Record baseline range, strength, endurance and power at the first loading appointment.
  3. Express each strength result as a percentage of normative expectation and note the test conditions.
  4. Set written criteria for the next stage before the athlete starts it.
  5. Use PSFS items drawn from the athlete's actual sport tasks and repeat them at every review.
  6. Do not progress on the calendar alone. If the criteria are not met, adjust the programme and retest.

Benchmark PS brings these measures into one place: range of movement from any inclinometer, strength from handheld dynamometry or gym lifts compared with normative data, endurance and power tests, the PSFS and the three-dimensional pain score, all tracked longitudinally. Where a result falls below the normative threshold, it suggests exercises with training parameters and progressions. If you are managing post-repair overhead athletes now, try writing criteria for one patient in these terms and see how the conversation with the surgeon and the athlete changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is time since surgery a reliable guide to return to sport after rotator cuff repair?

Time sets the protective phases and the earliest point at which loading can begin, but it does not show whether an athlete is ready. Consensus guidance supports progressing on measurable criteria, with the surgeon's timeline acting as a gate rather than the decision.

Which physical tests are useful for an overhead athlete after rotator cuff repair?

Range of movement with an inclinometer, strength with a handheld dynamometer or gym lifts expressed as a percentage of normative expectation, timed holds or repetition tests for endurance, and medicine ball throws measured with a tape measure for power.

How should psychological readiness be handled?

Psychological factors such as confidence and fear of re-injury are linked to return to sport. A practical approach is to record the athlete's own limited tasks on the Patient-Specific Functional Scale and track them alongside the physical results.
