Rotator Cuff Repair in Overhead Athletes: Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the harder return-to-sport problems in MSK practice. The tissue needs protection, the sport demands repeated high-velocity loading, and the evidence base is thinner than for the knee. Most clinicians are working from a surgeon's protocol, a calendar, and clinical impression. This article sets out what the shoulder literature supports, where it runs out, and how to build criteria you can measure and defend.

One caveat first. The evidence we can draw on here comes largely from consensus guidance and from related shoulder conditions, not from large trials of repaired cuffs in throwers or racquet athletes. We have tried to be clear about which is which.

Why calendar-based return is a weak foundation

Most post-operative protocols give approximate windows for immobilisation, passive motion, strengthening and sport-specific work. Those windows protect the repair, and you should respect the surgeon's restrictions on loading in the early phase. The problem comes later. Once the repair is protected, the question is no longer how long since surgery but what this athlete can now do.

The 2022 Bern consensus on shoulder injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport makes this point for the shoulder generally. It frames return to sport as a continuum of decisions informed by the athlete's status, rather than a single date, and it emphasises shared decision-making between clinician, athlete and surgeon (Schwank et al., 2022). That fits what we see in clinic. Two athletes at the same post-operative week can differ enormously in strength, range and confidence.

The same principle has been worked through in more detail for hamstring injury, where a multifactorial, criteria-based progression algorithm replaces time-based progression with performance milestones (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). The context is different, but the logic transfers. Progress when criteria are met, hold when they are not, and record what you measured so the next decision is not a guess.

What the evidence says about shoulder return to sport

Direct return-to-sport data for repaired rotator cuffs in overhead athletes are limited in the reference material we are working from, so it helps to look at neighbouring shoulder conditions that share the same decision problem.

A systematic review of non-surgical management of SLAP tears found that return to play is achievable for a meaningful proportion of athletes, but reporting of criteria and of how return was judged was inconsistent across studies (Steinmetz et al., 2022). A review of rehabilitation and return to sport after operative and non-operative treatment of anterior shoulder instability reaches a similar position: outcomes depend on the athlete's sport, level and the completeness of rehabilitation, and objective criteria are recommended over time alone (Herman et al., 2024).

The practical lesson is that the field has not settled on a validated, universal rotator cuff return-to-sport test battery. That is a reason to be explicit about the criteria you choose, not a reason to fall back on impression. If you cannot say what a green light looks like for this athlete before you start testing, you will end up rationalising whatever the day's session shows.

Building measurable criteria across four domains

A defensible set of criteria covers range, strength, endurance and power, plus the athlete's own account of function. Each of these can be captured with low-tech tools and compared against a stated expectation.

  • Range of movement. Measure elevation and rotation with an inclinometer, using the same position and the same tool each time. Overhead athletes often carry sport-adapted rotation profiles, so record the athlete's range against what is functionally required for their sport, not against a textbook figure alone.
  • Strength. Use handheld dynamometry for rotation and elevation, and gym-based lifts where the athlete is cleared to perform them. Express results as a percentage of the normative expectation for age, sex and weight. Handheld results compare against peer-reviewed normative data and gym lifts against Strengthlevel data. A single raw number tells you little; a percentage of expected tells you how far there is to go.
  • Endurance. Timed holds and maximum-repetition protocols matter because throwing and serving are repeated efforts. A shoulder that produces a good single effort and fades by the fortieth throw is not ready for match play.
  • Power. Seated or standing medicine ball throws, measured with a tape measure, give a practical upper-limb power measure. Introduce them only once the surgeon and your own loading criteria allow ballistic work.

Add two patient-reported measures. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale lets the athlete name the activities that matter, such as a serve, a throw from the outfield or a butterfly stroke, and rate them from 0 to 10. A three-dimensional pain score, the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration, captures more than a single pain rating, and it matters here because pain frequency and duration after loading often tell you more than peak intensity. Interpret change against the minimal clinically important difference rather than a hunch about whether things feel better.

Set a threshold for each domain in advance and write it down. Benchmark's output as a percentage of expected score gives you a common language across domains, so a stalled domain stands out at once.

Prognostic factors worth recording at baseline

Prognosis after cuff repair is shaped by factors that are largely fixed by the time the athlete reaches you, and others you can influence. Recording them at the outset lets you set realistic expectations and spot athletes who may need a longer runway.

  1. Tear and repair characteristics. Size, tissue quality and the surgeon's view of repair security determine how aggressively you can progress. Ask for this information in the operative note and let it set the ceiling on your loading, not the other way round.
  2. Pre-operative function. An athlete who arrives with a long history of weakness and loss of range has further to travel. Baseline strength and PSFS scores give you a starting point and a trajectory to compare against.
  3. Sport demands. A pitcher, a tennis player and a swimmer load the cuff differently. The criteria for a recreational overhead player are not the criteria for a professional thrower.
  4. Psychological factors. A systematic review of psychological factors in return to sport found that confidence, motivation and fear of re-injury are associated with whether and how athletes return (Ardern et al., 2013). Listen for this in the conversation, and note where PSFS items stay low despite adequate strength results. Benchmark does not score psychological readiness, so this is a clinical judgement you make in the room.
  5. Adherence and training age. Athletes who already train consistently tend to tolerate structured loading better. Ask what they were doing before injury.

None of these should be used to rule an athlete out. They help you decide how much of the plan to front-load with measurement and how closely to review progress.

Turning results into a progression decision

Test results are only useful if they change what you do. A reasonable structure is to retest at set intervals, compare each domain with its pre-agreed threshold, and decide among three actions: progress, hold, or revisit the plan.

If strength is below the normative threshold, the weakest domain drives the programme. Set sets, reps, load, rest and weekly volume per muscle group and movement pattern, and use progressions and regressions to move the athlete along. If strength is adequate but endurance lags, the problem is capacity under repetition and the programme should reflect that. If everything measures well but PSFS items remain low, look at confidence, technique demands and sport-specific exposure before you assume the shoulder is the limiting factor.

Staged exposure to sport is where overhead athletes are most often rushed. The Bern consensus supports progressive, criteria-informed exposure and close communication with the wider team (Schwank et al., 2022). In practice that means agreeing throwing or serving volumes with the coach, recording the athlete's response over the following 24 to 48 hours, and moving only when pain frequency and duration settle.

A written record matters for another reason. If a surgeon, coach or insurer later asks why the athlete was cleared, a table of dated measurements against stated thresholds is a far stronger answer than a note saying the shoulder looked good. Tracking results longitudinally, alongside PSFS and pain scores, makes the trajectory visible to everyone involved.

Next step

Frequently Asked Questions

When can an overhead athlete return to sport after rotator cuff repair?

There is no single validated date. Surgeon restrictions set the early limits, after which return should depend on measurable criteria such as range, strength as a percentage of expected, endurance and power, plus the athlete's PSFS and pain scores, rather than time since surgery alone.

Which tests are practical for assessing a shoulder after cuff repair?

Inclinometer range of movement, handheld dynamometry for strength compared against normative data, timed holds or maximum-repetition endurance tests, and medicine ball throws measured with a tape measure for power. All are low-cost and repeatable.

Do psychological factors affect return to sport after shoulder surgery?

Evidence from systematic review across sports injuries links confidence, motivation and fear of re-injury to return-to-sport outcomes. Clinicians should explore these in conversation, as Benchmark PS does not score psychological readiness.
