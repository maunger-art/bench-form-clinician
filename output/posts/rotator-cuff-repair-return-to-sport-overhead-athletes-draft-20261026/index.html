Rotator Cuff Repair: Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors for Overhead Athletes

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the harder return-to-sport problems in MSK practice. The tissue needs protecting for months, yet the demands at the end of rehab (throwing, serving, spiking, swimming, pressing) are some of the highest the shoulder will ever see. The decision to clear someone is often made on time since surgery, a surgeon's protocol, and a clinician's impression of how the shoulder looks. That is a thin basis for a decision with a real re-tear cost.

This article sets out what the evidence supports, where it runs out, and how to build return-to-sport criteria from measurable, comparable data rather than impression.

What the evidence base actually gives you

Be clear about the limits first. The shoulder return-to-sport literature is smaller and less consistent than the ACL literature, and most high-quality consensus guidance is written for shoulder injury in general, not rotator cuff repair specifically. The 2022 Bern consensus statement is the most useful single anchor. It frames shoulder rehabilitation and return to sport as a staged, criteria-led process in which progression depends on the athlete meeting function and capacity targets, not simply on elapsed time (Schwank et al., 2022).

That framing matters because it separates two questions that are often blurred:

  • What is the tissue ready for? This is largely governed by the surgeon's protocol, repair size and quality, and time since surgery.
  • What can the athlete actually do? This is a clinical measurement question, and it is the one you can answer in clinic.

Time since surgery tells you the first. Only testing tells you the second. Neither replaces the other, and a clinician who clears an athlete on a calendar date has only answered half the question.

Prognostic factors worth recording at baseline

Several factors are commonly used to judge how an overhead athlete is likely to do after repair. Most of the structural ones come from the operating surgeon and imaging, not from you, so ask for them and record them: tear size and pattern, tissue quality, whether the repair was partial or full thickness, and any associated labral or biceps pathology. These shape how conservative the early phases should be and how realistic the end point is.

The factors you can influence or measure yourself are more useful for planning:

  1. Pre-injury level and sport demand. A recreational swimmer and a professional baseball pitcher are not returning to the same task. Define the target movement and workload before you define the criteria.
  2. Baseline strength relative to expectation. Where the athlete started, and how far below a normative expectation they sit, sets the size of the job.
  3. Patient-reported function. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) captures the activities that matter to this athlete, rated 0 to 10, and gives you a baseline that is specific to their sport tasks.
  4. Pain profile. A three-dimensional pain score, the mean of frequency, intensity and duration, is more informative than a single 0 to 10 number because pain at night and pain with activity behave differently.
  5. Confidence and self-efficacy. Athletes who doubt the shoulder will hold up tend to under-load or avoid it. The evidence here comes mostly from other injuries, but a critically appraised topic on imagery suggests it can raise self-efficacy for rehabilitation tasks after sport injury (Singleton et al., 2023). Treat it as a reasonable adjunct, not a proven rotator cuff intervention.

Be wary of anything that sounds like a prognostic score built from a single variable. In this population, no single measure should carry the decision.

Building criteria from measurable capacity

The Bern consensus supports a criteria-based approach to progression and return to sport across the shoulder (Schwank et al., 2022). In practice, that means writing down, before the athlete reaches the late phase, exactly what they must demonstrate. A workable set for an overhead athlete after rotator cuff repair covers four domains.

Range of movement

Record elevation and rotation with any inclinometer. The tool matters less than using the same one, in the same position, every time, so the numbers are comparable across visits. For throwers, compare the measured range with what is expected for the sport and for the athlete's own baseline before injury if you have it.

Strength

Handheld dynamometry for external rotation, internal rotation and elevation is cheap and quick. Express the result as a percentage of normative expectation for the athlete's age, sex and weight, using peer-reviewed normative data. This tells you how far the athlete sits from where a healthy person of their profile would be, which is a more honest target than a number set by habit. For later phases, gym-based pressing and pulling loads can be compared against Strengthlevel normative data in the same way.

The relationship between rotation strengths has its own article in this series, so it is not repeated here. The point for this topic is that strength is judged against an expectation, not against a rule of thumb.

Endurance

Overhead athletes rarely fail on one maximal effort. They fail on the fortieth throw or the last length of the pool. Timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols for the cuff and scapular muscles give you a measure of fatigue resistance that a single strength test misses. Record the repetitions or hold duration, repeat under the same conditions, and look at the trend.

Power

Throwing and striking are power tasks. Seated or standing medicine ball throws for distance, measured with a tape measure, give you a cheap and repeatable power measure for the upper limb. Use a consistent ball mass, position and number of attempts. Introduce it only when earlier criteria are met and the surgeon's protocol allows loaded ballistic work.

None of these measures needs expensive hardware. All of them can be tracked longitudinally and viewed alongside PSFS and the pain score, which is what turns a set of test results into a decision.

Why strength criteria alone will mislead you

It is tempting to treat a strong dynamometer reading as proof of readiness. Evidence from other joints shows why that is risky. A meta-analysis of randomised trials in ACL reconstruction found that strengthening improves muscle strength and performance but does not improve pain (Bafrouei et al., 2025). Different joint, different surgery, but the lesson transfers as a caution: capacity and symptoms can move independently, so you need both sets of data.

Similarly, work on late-stage ACL rehabilitation showed that targeted strength training can shift athletes towards their return-to-sport test targets (Stojanović et al., 2023). That is a reminder that criteria are only useful if you then train towards them. A test that shows a deficit should lead to a specific training prescription with defined sets, repetitions, load, rest and weekly volume, not a general instruction to keep strengthening.

Equally, passing every capacity test does not settle whether the athlete is ready for the sport itself. A staged return, with graded exposure to throwing or overhead volume, remains the bridge between clinic criteria and competition. The Bern consensus describes return to sport as a continuum from participation through to performance, not a single clearance event (Schwank et al., 2022).

A practical decision checklist

Before you clear an overhead athlete to return to full participation, you should be able to answer yes to each of the following, with numbers behind the answer:

  • Has the surgeon confirmed the repair is ready for the loading demands of the sport?
  • Is range of movement recorded with a consistent tool and appropriate for the sport?
  • Are strength scores at an agreed percentage of normative expectation for the athlete's age, sex and weight?
  • Has endurance been tested with a repeatable timed or repetition protocol, and is it improving?
  • Has a power measure such as medicine ball distance been recorded and tracked?
  • Is the PSFS score on the athlete's chosen sport tasks within the change you would expect, judged against the minimal clinically important difference?
  • Is the three-dimensional pain score low and stable under the loads you have reached?

You set the thresholds, ideally with the surgeon and the athlete, before testing. Deciding afterwards is how criteria drift to fit the result you wanted.

If the athlete misses a criterion, the answer is a targeted block of training and a retest, with the same protocol, not a reduced threshold.

Next step

If your current shoulder pathway relies on time since surgery and clinical impression, start by choosing three measures you can repeat reliably: an inclinometer range, a handheld dynamometer strength test expressed against a normative expectation, and the PSFS. Record them at every visit for your next five rotator cuff patients. The judgement stays with you. The data makes it easier to defend.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long after rotator cuff repair can an overhead athlete return to sport?

There is no single safe date. Time since surgery guides what the tissue can tolerate, and the surgeon's protocol sets the limits. Return to sport should also depend on measured capacity such as range, strength against normative expectation, endurance and power, with patient-reported function and pain tracked alongside.

Can I test shoulder strength after rotator cuff repair without expensive equipment?

Yes. A handheld dynamometer gives repeatable measures of external rotation, internal rotation and elevation. Express results as a percentage of normative expectation for age, sex and weight, and use the same position and protocol at every visit.

Which patient-reported measures are useful after rotator cuff repair?

The Patient-Specific Functional Scale lets the athlete rate the sport tasks that matter to them from 0 to 10. A three-dimensional pain score, the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration, gives a fuller picture than a single pain rating.
