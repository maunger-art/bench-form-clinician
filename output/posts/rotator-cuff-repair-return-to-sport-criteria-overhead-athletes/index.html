Rotator Cuff Repair: Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors for Overhead Athletes

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the hardest return-to-sport decisions in MSK practice. The tissue has to heal, but the shoulder also has to tolerate repeated high-velocity loading in a position that stressed it in the first place. Most of us were taught to progress by protocol. The surgeon sets the early restrictions, and after that the calendar often does the rest.

A calendar tells you how long ago the surgery was. It tells you nothing about whether this athlete can throw, serve or swim at the level they need. This article sets out what the evidence supports, where it runs thin, and how to build criteria you can measure and defend.

What the evidence base can and cannot tell you

Be honest about the limits. Rotator cuff repair in overhead athletes is a small, heterogeneous population, and return-to-sport research in the shoulder is thinner than in the knee. The knee literature is a useful comparison because it has spent two decades on criteria-based progression, and even there the field still argues about which criteria predict outcome (Glattke et al., 2022).

The most useful shoulder-specific source is the 2022 Bern Consensus Statement, which covers injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport across participation levels (Schwank et al., 2022). It is a consensus document, not trial evidence, so treat it as a structure for your reasoning rather than a set of validated cut-offs. Its value is that it frames return to sport as a staged process with the sport's demands in view, and not as a single discharge event.

Closely related work on non-surgical management of SLAP tears in athletes found that return to play was commonly achieved in the studies reviewed (Steinmetz et al., 2022). That is a different pathology from a repaired cuff, so do not transfer the rates. The relevance is the reminder that shoulder return-to-play data are reported inconsistently, and that definitions of return vary between studies. When you quote an expected outcome to an athlete, say which population it comes from.

Practical takeaway

  • Distinguish return to participation, return to sport and return to previous performance. Agree which one you are aiming for at the start.
  • Quote outcome estimates cautiously and name the source population.
  • Use consensus frameworks to structure decisions, then fill the structure with measurements from your own patient.

Prognostic factors that shape the conversation

Some prognostic factors are fixed by the time the athlete reaches you. Tear size, tissue quality, whether the tear was traumatic or degenerative, and the surgical technique are largely out of your hands. Your job is to understand them from the operation note and the surgeon, and to set expectations accordingly. A small, acute tear in a young thrower and a larger, chronic tear in a masters-level tennis player are not the same rehabilitation problem, even if both are described as a cuff repair.

Other factors are modifiable, and these are where measurement earns its place. Strength deficits, restricted range, low overall conditioning and a pain profile that is not settling all shape how the athlete responds to loading. Psychological factors also matter. A systematic review of returning to sport after injury found that confidence, fear of re-injury and motivation were consistently associated with whether athletes returned (Ardern et al., 2013). That review was not shoulder-specific, so apply it as a general principle. In practice it means listening to what the athlete says about trusting the shoulder, and noting whether their own functional goals are being met.

This is where the Patient-Specific Functional Scale is useful. Ask the athlete to name the activities they need back, for example a full-effort serve, a overhead press at a given load, or a butterfly stroke over a full set, and score each from 0 to 10. Those items become your target list, and they are far more specific to this athlete than a generic shoulder questionnaire. Benchmark PS provides the minimal clinically important difference and expected rate of change alongside PSFS scores, so you can tell whether a change is real or within noise.

Practical takeaway

  • Record fixed prognostic factors from the operation note at the first appointment, so they sit beside your measurements.
  • Use PSFS to capture the athlete's own overhead tasks, and review the same items at every re-test.
  • Track the three-dimensional pain score (frequency, intensity and duration) rather than a single intensity rating. A shoulder that hurts less often and for less time after loading is telling you something a peak score hides.

Why time since surgery is a weak criterion

Early rehabilitation protects the repair, and the surgeon's restrictions are not negotiable. Once those restrictions lift, time alone becomes a poor guide. Two athletes at the same point after surgery can differ enormously in strength, range and tolerance to load.

Criteria-based progression has been developed most thoroughly in other regions. In hamstring injury, a multifactorial algorithm progresses the athlete through phases using clinical and functional criteria in place of fixed durations (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). In the knee, systematic review evidence on reconstruction rehabilitation highlights the move toward criteria-based approaches while acknowledging the variability in how they are defined (Glattke et al., 2022). The shoulder can borrow the logic without borrowing the specific tests: each phase has an entry gate made of measurable results, and the athlete moves on when they meet it.

The same applies at discharge. A date on the calendar is a poor reason to release an athlete to throwing, and a poor reason to hold them back. Measurable criteria protect both the athlete and you, because the decision can be explained and, if needed, defended.

Building measurable criteria with basic equipment

Benchmark PS is technology-agnostic, so you can build a shoulder battery from tools most clinics already own. The four domains map across as follows.

  1. Range of movement. Use any inclinometer to record elevation and rotation, and enter the values alongside the expected range for the athlete. Throwers often have sport-specific rotation patterns, so interpret the numbers in the context of the athlete's history and the surgeon's guidance.
  2. Strength. Use a handheld dynamometer for shoulder rotation and elevation, compared against peer-reviewed normative data and expressed as a percentage of the expected score for age, sex and weight. Once the athlete is cleared for gym loading, record lifts such as an overhead press or a row. These are compared with Strengthlevel normative data. Either way, the output is a percentage of normative expectation, which gives you a target you can set before the athlete reaches it.
  3. Endurance. Use timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols suited to the shoulder, such as a set number of controlled repetitions at a fixed load. Overhead sport is repetitive, so a shoulder that scores well on a single maximal effort but fades quickly is not ready for a training session.
  4. Power. Medicine ball throws measured with a tape measure are cheap and sport-relevant. Seated and standing throws, overhead and chest-pass, give you a distance that can be compared against expectation and repeated at each review. Smartphone apps such as MyJump2 are useful for lower-limb jumps if the athlete's sport needs it, and a force plate is not required.

These results are only useful when the protocol is identical each time. Fix the position, the instructions, the number of trials and the device, and record them. A handheld dynamometer reading taken in a different position at each visit cannot be compared with the last one.

Practical takeaway

  • Choose three or four tests that map onto the athlete's PSFS items and run the same protocol at each re-test.
  • Set the target as a percentage of normative expectation before testing, so the criterion is not decided after you see the number.
  • Do not progress on a single metric. Strength, endurance, power, range and PSFS should move together before you advance throwing or serving volume.

From criteria to a graded return

The Bern consensus approach emphasises progressive exposure to sport-specific demands (Schwank et al., 2022). In practice this means moving from general strength and endurance, to loaded overhead work, to power and throwing-type tasks, to sport-specific volume and intensity. At each step, the previous step's results act as the gate.

When a test falls below normative expectation, the next step is exercise selection and dosage. Benchmark PS suggests exercises when a result is below the normative threshold and recommends sets, reps, load, rest and frequency, with progressions and regressions and weekly volume by muscle group and movement pattern. That keeps the rehabilitation plan tied to the measured deficit instead of to a generic shoulder programme.

Expect to re-test regularly and to see the plan change. If strength has reached expectation but endurance has not, the next block should reflect that. If power lags, throwing intensity waits. If PSFS items are not improving despite good physical scores, go back and ask the athlete what is still limiting them. The measurements prompt the question, and your clinical judgement answers it.

Finally, document the reasoning. Benchmark PS tracks objective data and PROMs longitudinally, and its audio transcription can help you structure clinical notes after the session. A clear record of what was measured, what the criterion was and why you made the decision is the strongest evidence that the return was considered and not guessed.

A natural next step

If you treat overhead athletes after cuff repair, pick one current patient and write down their three PSFS items, the four tests you would use, and the percentage of expected score you would want before each stage of return. If you cannot fill that in, you have found where your current process relies on time and impression. Frequently Asked Questions

What criteria should an overhead athlete meet before returning to sport after rotator cuff repair?

There are no validated universal cut-offs. A defensible approach uses measurable criteria across range of movement, strength, endurance and power, expressed as a percentage of normative expectation, alongside PSFS and pain scores that show the athlete's own overhead tasks are recovering. The surgeon's guidance on tissue healing always applies first.

Is time since surgery a reliable return-to-sport criterion?

No. It protects the repair in the early phase, but afterwards athletes at the same point differ widely in strength, endurance and tolerance to load. Criteria-based progression, as used in other regions such as the hamstring and knee, uses measured results as the gate between phases.

Which tests can be used without expensive equipment?

An inclinometer for range, a handheld dynamometer for strength, timed holds or maximum-repetition tests for endurance, and medicine ball throws measured with a tape measure for power. The protocol must be identical at every re-test so results can be compared.
