Rotator Cuff Repair: Return-to-Sport Criteria and Prognostic Factors for Overhead Athletes

Rotator cuff repair in an overhead athlete is one of the hardest return-to-sport calls in MSK practice. The tissue has to heal, but the athlete also has to throw, serve or swing repeatedly at speed. The evidence base is thinner than for the knee, and many clinics fall back on a calendar and a surgeon's protocol. That is a weak basis for a decision that determines whether someone returns to competition or re-tears.

This article sets out what the shoulder literature supports, which prognostic factors should shape your expectations, and how to build return-to-sport criteria that are measurable rather than time-based.

Why time since surgery is a poor gate

Surgical protocols give you tissue-protection windows, and those matter in the early phase. They tell you when loading is permitted. They do not tell you whether the shoulder can tolerate the demands of the sport. Two athletes at the same post-operative point can differ enormously in strength, range and confidence.

The 2022 Bern Consensus Statement on shoulder injury prevention, rehabilitation and return to sport sets out a framework for athletes at all participation levels (Schwank et al., 2022). Its central message is that return to sport is a continuum and a decision shared between clinician and athlete, informed by measured capacity rather than elapsed time. That reasoning applies directly to post-repair rehabilitation.

The same principle has been formalised in other regions. Mendiguchia and colleagues described a multifactorial, criteria-based progressive algorithm for hamstring injury, where progression depends on meeting defined functional criteria rather than days passed (Mendiguchia et al., 2017). It is a different joint and a different injury, but the logic transfers: define the criteria, measure them, progress when they are met.

Practical takeaway: write down the criteria for each phase before the athlete reaches it, and record the number, not a judgement of whether it looked good.

Prognostic factors to establish at the start

Before you set targets, set expectations. Factors commonly considered in the surgical literature include tear size and number of tendons involved, the quality of the remaining muscle and tendon, age, pre-injury level and the demands of the sport. We have not drawn specific effect sizes for these from the reference set for this article, so treat the list as a prompt for discussion with the surgeon rather than a prediction tool.

What you can do in clinic is capture the athlete's baseline as precisely as possible:

  • Range of movement measured with an inclinometer, recorded in the same positions each visit.
  • Strength measured with a handheld dynamometer in standardised positions, expressed as a percentage of the normative expectation for the athlete's age, sex and weight.
  • Patient-identified limitations on the Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS), which for an overhead athlete will usually name the specific task (the serve, the throw, the overhead lift) rather than a generic activity.
  • The three-dimensional pain score, the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration, which separates a shoulder that hurts occasionally and briefly from one that hurts often and for long periods.

An athlete whose pre-operative strength was already low, or whose PSFS items were already severely limited, starts the rehabilitation from a different place. Knowing that early prevents the common mistake of attributing a slow trajectory to the repair when it reflects the starting point.

What the neighbouring shoulder evidence tells us

Direct evidence on return to sport after rotator cuff repair in overhead athletes is limited, so it is reasonable to look at related shoulder conditions with the caveat that the pathology differs.

A systematic review of return to play after nonsurgical management of SLAP tears found that return was achievable for many athletes without surgery, which reinforces the value of structured rehabilitation as a primary or complementary pathway (Steinmetz et al., 2022). A review of rehabilitation and return to sport after operative and nonoperative treatment of anterior shoulder instability also highlights that return decisions should rest on functional recovery and not on the treatment pathway alone (Herman et al., 2024).

The common thread across these sources is that the outcome depends on what the shoulder can do, measured against the demands of the sport. The route to that point matters less than whether the athlete can demonstrate the capacity.

Practical takeaway: do not borrow a criterion from another shoulder condition without checking that it makes sense for a repaired cuff. Borrow the method, which is measurable criteria agreed in advance.

Building measurable return-to-sport criteria

Benchmark PS organises physical performance into four domains. Each one maps onto a part of the overhead athlete's return-to-sport profile.

Range of movement

Inclinometer measurements of the movements the sport requires, taken with whatever inclinometer tool you already use. Compare against the athlete's expected range and track the trend. The question is whether the range needed for the sport is available, not whether it matches a textbook number.

Strength

Handheld dynamometer testing of the shoulder musculature, expressed as a percentage of normative expectation from peer-reviewed literature. For the athlete's general strength base, gym-based lifts can be compared with Strengthlevel normative data. Set the target as a percentage of expected score, agree it with the athlete and the surgeon, and retest at fixed intervals using the same position and the same dynamometer.

Endurance

Overhead sport is repetitive. A single maximal effort tells you little about whether the shoulder will tolerate a full session. Timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols give a measurable endurance figure that can be tracked and compared as rehabilitation progresses.

Power

Throwing is a power task. A seated or standing medicine ball throw measured with a tape measure is cheap, repeatable and sport-relevant. It should be introduced only once the earlier criteria have been met and the surgeon has cleared loading at speed. Record distance with the same ball weight and the same technique each time.

Alongside these, the PSFS should show the athlete's own priority tasks moving toward the top of the scale, and the pain score should be low and falling. MCID and expected rate of change give you context for deciding whether a PSFS change is meaningful or within measurement noise.

The psychological side without a questionnaire

A systematic review of psychological factors associated with returning to sport after injury found that psychological readiness is a significant contributor to whether athletes return, alongside physical recovery (Ardern et al., 2013). That matters for overhead athletes, who may hesitate at the point of full-speed throwing even when the numbers look good.

Benchmark PS does not include a psychological-readiness tool, and you do not need one to act on this. The PSFS gives the athlete a structured way to rate their own ability on the tasks that matter to them, and a gap between strong physical results and a low PSFS score is a conversation worth having. Ask what specifically is limiting the task, and use the answer to shape the next stage of loading.

Putting it into a clinic workflow

  1. At the first post-operative contact, record baseline PSFS items, the three-dimensional pain score, and whatever range and strength can be tested safely.
  2. Agree the criteria for each phase with the surgeon and the athlete, in terms of percentage of expected score rather than dates.
  3. Retest on a fixed schedule using identical positions and equipment.
  4. Introduce endurance and power testing as loading permissions allow.
  5. Make the return-to-sport decision jointly, with the longitudinal data in front of everyone.

The benefit for a clinic is consistency. When every athlete is assessed the same way, you can compare across your caseload, explain decisions to surgeons and coaches, and show where a rehabilitation pathway is working and where it is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should return to sport after rotator cuff repair be based on time since surgery?

Time determines when loading is permitted to protect healing tissue, but it should not be the gate for return to sport. Criteria should be measurable capacity such as range, strength as a percentage of expected score, endurance and throwing power, agreed in advance with the surgeon and athlete.

Which tests suit an overhead athlete after cuff repair?

Inclinometer range of movement, handheld dynamometer strength compared with normative data, timed hold or maximum-repetition endurance tests, and medicine ball throw distance measured with a tape measure, alongside PSFS and the three-dimensional pain score.

How does Benchmark PS handle the psychological side of return to sport?

It does not include a psychological-readiness questionnaire. The PSFS lets athletes rate their own ability on the tasks that matter to them, and a mismatch between good physical results and a low PSFS score is a prompt for clinical discussion.
