Simple Strength Tests That Predict Long-Term Health, Not Just Recovery

Objective Assessment strength testing outcome measurement long-term health objective assessment

Strength tests used for rehab milestones also flag long-term musculoskeletal health risk. Why physios should track them past discharge, not just to it.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Most strength tests in a physiotherapy clinic exist to answer one question: has this patient recovered enough to be discharged, or cleared to return to sport? Quadriceps force after a knee reconstruction, external rotation torque after a cuff repair, sit-to-stand time after a joint replacement, all get measured against a threshold, a box gets ticked, and the patient moves on. What gets lost in that framing is that the same number, tracked over a longer horizon, tells you something different: whether this person is building durable musculoskeletal capacity or just clearing a bar low enough to walk out the door.

The rehab literature is built almost entirely around short-term milestones. Discharge criteria, return-to-play windows, twelve-week outcome measures. That's understandable given how trials are funded and reported, but it leaves a gap between what we measure and what actually determines whether a patient is still doing well two or five years later. This article looks at what the strength data we already collect can tell us about that longer arc, and what changes if you start treating strength testing as a health marker rather than a discharge gate.

The same test, two different jobs

Take quadriceps strength after total knee arthroplasty. The standard framing is functional: can the patient manage stairs, get out of a chair unaided, walk without a limp. Systematic review evidence on early physiotherapy after TKA consistently reports these outcomes, pain, range of motion, and quality of life, as the primary endpoints clinicians are chasing (Aftab et al., 2025). That's the right thing to measure for a discharge decision. But strength deficits that persist past the point of "good enough for daily function" are also the deficits associated with slower long-term joint loading tolerance and a higher chance of the other knee deteriorating under compensatory load. The test doesn't change. The window you read it over does.

Part of the problem is that the outcome measures used across TKA rehabilitation trials are themselves inconsistent. A review of study designs and outcome measures in TKA rehabilitation found wide variation in what gets reported and how, which makes it difficult to build the kind of longitudinal, cross-study benchmark data that would let a clinician say "this strength trajectory at six months predicts this joint health outcome at three years" (Dávila et al., 2019). Without standardised measurement, the data that could answer the long-term question simply doesn't get collected in a form anyone can pool.

This is the same problem Benchmark PS was built to address, not by inventing new tests, but by making sure the tests clinicians already run produce numbers that are comparable across patients, across clinicians, and across time. A grip strength reading or a sit-to-stand time is only useful as a long-term marker if it's captured the same way at month one and month twelve and the same way between all clinicians who test it.

What happens after the rehab episode ends

Strength testing almost always stops at discharge, which means the period where deconditioning is most likely to creep back in goes completely unmonitored. This matters because the interventions used to build strength during rehab, particularly blood flow restriction training, exist precisely because standard heavy loading isn't tolerated in the early phase. A systematic review of BFR after ACL injury found it produces comparable or better strength outcomes than standard loading protocols in patients who can't yet tolerate high mechanical load (Colombo et al., 2024), and the broader BFR literature across musculoskeletal rehabilitation shows consistent strength gains using loads far below what would normally be required to drive adaptation (Hughes et al., 2017).

The clinical takeaway isn't just "BFR works." It's that strength can be built and preserved even when the joint itself isn't ready for heavy load, which means there's rarely a good excuse for a strength curve to be left untracked during that period. If a clinic is measuring quad strength at the start of a BFR block and again at the end, that same measurement infrastructure can keep running past discharge, at three months, six months, a year, cheaply, because the tool and the protocol already exist.

Open kinetic chain quadriceps work during early ACL rehabilitation shows a similar pattern. A recent meta-analysis found OKC exercise in the first weeks after reconstruction doesn't compromise graft safety and supports earlier strength gains than protocols that avoid it entirely (Fontanier et al., 2025). That earlier strength gain matters less for the three-month return-to-sport clearance than it does for the patient's baseline joint capacity five years later, particularly given how strongly quadriceps weakness after ACL reconstruction is linked to earlier onset of knee osteoarthritis. The test that clears a patient for sport in month nine is the same test that, tracked forward, flags whether that knee is heading toward degenerative change.

Standardisation gaps across conditions make long-term benchmarking hard

The pattern holds outside the knee. Rehabilitation protocols following rotator cuff repair vary substantially between surgeons and services, and a meta-analysis of current evidence found meaningful differences in loading timelines, immobilisation duration, and strength testing schedules across studies (Bandara et al., 2021). That variation isn't just an academic problem. It means two patients with clinically identical repairs can leave rehab with completely different strength trajectories, and neither the clinician nor the patient has a reliable reference point for what "normal for this stage" should look like a year down the line.

Meniscus repair rehabilitation shows the same inconsistency. A review of postoperative protocols following arthroscopic meniscus repair found substantial disagreement in weight-bearing restrictions and return-to-activity criteria between services (Harput et al., 2020). Where protocols disagree this much on the short-term milestones, there's effectively no chance of comparable long-term strength data existing across clinics. This is precisely why benchmark data in this field tends to come from athletic performance and sports science literature rather than clinical trials. Athletes get tested repeatedly, using consistent protocols, over years, which is exactly the kind of longitudinal picture MSK rehab trials rarely produce.

For a clinic without access to sports-science-grade equipment, the practical fix isn't waiting for the research to catch up. It's picking a small set of simple, repeatable tests, sit-to-stand time, grip strength, single-leg strength ratios, and running them on a fixed schedule that extends well past discharge, so that individual clinics start building their own longitudinal benchmark even while the wider literature remains fragmented.

Remote and digital tracking extends the window

One barrier to tracking strength past discharge is simply logistics. Patients stop coming in once they feel better, and clinics don't have the appointment slots to bring them back purely for a strength recheck. Digital and remote-monitoring models offer a partial answer. A randomised trial comparing digital to conventional physiotherapy for chronic shoulder pain found the digital pathway produced comparable clinical outcomes with substantially more data points captured over time, because patients could log function and complete guided reassessments between visits rather than only at scheduled appointments (Pak et al., 2023).

That model translates directly to strength monitoring. A patient who's been discharged doesn't need a full clinic appointment to repeat a sit-to-stand test or a strength reading if the protocol is simple and standardised enough to run unsupervised or with minimal guidance. This is where Benchmarks gym based tests can be immensely useful. By getting the patient to test themselves in their own environment, clinicians can essentially outsource testing to the patient to collect longitudinal data without the time burden.

Building a longer view into routine practice

None of this requires new equipment or a research grant. It requires treating tests as data points on a longer curve rather than pass/fail gates on a shorter one.

  • Pick two or three simple tests you already use (sit-to-stand, plank, knee extension strength for example) and commit to a fixed retest schedule that runs past discharge, not just up to it.
  • Standardise the protocol before you standardise the interpretation. Position, warm-up, number of reps, and equipment need to be identical every time or the trend line is noise.
  • Use BFR-supported early strength data as a baseline, not a discharge target, when patients can't tolerate heavy load early on (Colombo et al., 2024) (Hughes et al., 2017).
  • Flag persistent asymmetry at discharge as a long-term risk marker, not just a discharge criterion, and schedule a follow-up recheck rather than closing the episode outright.
  • Where a full clinic visit isn't justified, use simple remote check-ins to keep the data flowing between episodes of care (Pak et al., 2023).

The strength number a physiotherapist reads at week six was never just about week six. It's the earliest data point in a trajectory that keeps running long after the discharge letter is written, and clinics that treat it that way end up with a genuinely different kind of evidence base than the one generated by testing purely to clear a milestone.

Where standardised measurement fits

None of this depends on expensive kit. It depends on consistency: the same test, run the same way, by whoever happens to be in the room, at intervals that extend past the point where most services stop looking. Benchmark PS exists to make that consistency achievable in a normal clinic workflow, so the strength data collected for a discharge decision can also become the longitudinal record that tells you something about a patient's musculoskeletal trajectory years later. If your clinic already runs strength tests as part of standard care, the next step isn't a new test. It's a schedule that doesn't stop at discharge.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should physiotherapists keep testing strength after a patient is discharged?

Where feasible, yes. Strength tests used to clear discharge or return-to-sport criteria also carry information about longer-term joint and muscle health. A simple, standardised recheck at three, six and twelve months costs little and captures a trajectory that discharge testing alone misses.

Which strength tests are simplest to track long-term without special equipment?

Sit-to-stand time, grip strength, and single-leg strength ratios are all low-tech, quick to administer, and repeatable across visits, which makes them practical choices for tracking beyond the initial rehab episode.

Does blood flow restriction training data have any relevance to long-term health tracking?

BFR protocols already generate repeated strength measurements during early rehab when heavy loading isn't tolerated. That existing measurement infrastructure can be extended past discharge at little extra cost, turning short-term rehab data into a longer-term strength record.

Why is there so little long-term strength benchmark data in clinical MSK research?

Reducing Treatment Variation Between Clinicians: What Standardised Testing Changes

Objective Assessment treatment variation standardised testing objective assessment clinical consistency

Why identical patients get different care from different physiotherapists, and how standardised testing closes that gap without removing clinical judgement.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Two physiotherapists see the same patient with the same knee pain in the same week, and there's a fair chance they run different tests, apply different cut-offs, and reach different conclusions about what counts as ready. That's not a hypothetical. It's the default state of most MSK services, because strength, range of movement and functional capacity are still judged largely by feel. One clinician's grade 4 out of 5 on manual muscle testing is another's grade 4+. One decides a patient can progress load based on how the session felt; another wants a specific number under load before agreeing. None of this reflects a lack of skill. It reflects the absence of a shared, objective reference point that every clinician in the building is working from.

Standardised testing doesn't take clinical judgement out of the room. It gives that judgement something consistent to sit on. When every clinician in a service measures the same variable, the same way, against the same normative reference, the variation that remains is the variation that should exist: different patients, different presentations, different goals. The variation that shouldn't exist, two clinicians reaching different conclusions from identical information, gets squeezed out.

Where clinician-to-clinician variation actually comes from

Most variation between clinicians doesn't come from disagreement about diagnosis. It comes from the measurement step in between diagnosis and decision. Manual muscle testing grades are ordinal and rater-dependent. Goniometry readings drift between examiners by several degrees even with training. Functional criteria like "ready to progress" or "safe to load" are frequently a clinical impression rather than a number, which means the same patient can get a green light from one clinician and a hold from another, on the same day, with no change in presentation.

This matters more in services built around clinician autonomy at first contact. A systematic review of direct access physiotherapy, where patients reach a physiotherapist without a prior referral or gatekeeping consultation, found outcomes hold up well when clinicians exercise full clinical judgement from the first appointment (Gallotti et al., 2023). That's a genuinely positive finding for the profession, but it also raises the stakes on consistency. If there's no second clinician checking the reasoning, the quality of the initial assessment carries the whole episode of care. A service where ten clinicians assess the same presentation ten different ways isn't protected by autonomy, it's exposed by it.

None of this is really about individual competence. It's a structural gap: most physiotherapy training teaches manual assessment technique thoroughly and objective benchmarking barely at all, so clinicians default to whatever heuristics they picked up in their first few years of practice. Multiply that across a team of eight or ten clinicians trained in different rotations, under different supervisors, and the spread in judgement calls becomes wide enough that two patients with identical presentations can end up on measurably different care pathways.

What changes when the test itself is standardised

The fix isn't a new test. It's the same tests, run the same way, scored against the same reference every time, regardless of which clinician is in the room. Range of movement captured with a consistent inclinometer protocol. Endurance assessed with a fixed timed-hold or max-rep format. Strength captured either through handheld dynamometry, benchmarked against peer-reviewed normative data, or through gym-based lifts benchmarked against Strengthlevel.com population data, expressed as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight. Power assessed through jump or throw tests using nothing more exotic than a smartphone app, a medicine ball or a tape measure.

The output that matters here isn't the raw number. It's the percentage of normative expectation. A clinician reading "68% of expected strength for age and sex" doesn't need to argue with a colleague about whether that's a grade 4 or a grade 4+. The number does the work that used to depend on two people agreeing on a subjective scale. Paired with the Patient-Specific Functional Scale and a three-dimensional pain score covering frequency, intensity and duration, the picture a clinician is working from stops being "what did this feel like in the room" and starts being "where does this patient sit against an expected trajectory, and how has that shifted since the last visit."

That shift is what actually reduces variation. It's not that the objective number is infallible, it's that every clinician in the service is reading the same number, generated the same way, so disagreements move from "what grade is this" to "given this data, what should we do about it", which is a far more productive conversation and one that's easier to audit.

The evidence base itself hasn't settled on one protocol either

Clinician variation isn't purely a training or discipline problem. It's partly downstream of an evidence base that hasn't converged on single protocols either, which leaves clinicians choosing between genuinely competing, evidence-supported approaches. A trial comparing two well-established eccentric loading protocols for chronic Achilles tendinopathy head to head found both are defensible options with different loading structures, rather than one clearly superior regime (Habets et al., 2017). When even the trial literature offers two valid roads, individual clinicians default to whichever one they trained under, and that choice alone creates variation in what "standard treatment" looks like across a team.

The same pattern shows up at scale. A synthesis of 55 randomised controlled trials covering more than 4,300 patients receiving physical therapy after lumbar disc herniation surgery had to pool a genuinely heterogeneous set of rehabilitation protocols to draw any conclusions at all (Brotis et al., 2025). A systematic review of physiotherapy after lumbar fusion surgery found a similarly fragmented picture, with wide differences in timing, intensity and content of rehabilitation programmes across the included studies, and correspondingly limited ability to say which specific protocol elements were driving outcomes (Madera et al., 2017).

That heterogeneity in the published evidence doesn't excuse variation in the clinic, but it does explain where a chunk of it comes from. If the research hasn't settled the protocol question, clinicians are left making individual judgement calls about programme design on top of individual judgement calls about assessment. Standardised testing can't resolve the first problem, the field will keep debating loading protocols for years. What it can do is stop the second problem, inconsistent measurement, from compounding the first.

Consistency changes decisions, not just documentation

The case for standardisation isn't purely about tidier notes. It's about whether the treatment decision made on the back of a test is the same decision a colleague would have made from the same data. There's a reasonable evidence base showing that structured, software-supported delivery models hold their own against traditional face-to-face judgement calls, which is a useful proof of concept for the same principle applied to assessment.

A meta-analysis of digital healthcare-supported rehabilitation across musculoskeletal conditions found meaningful improvements in pain and quality-of-life outcomes when care was delivered through structured digital protocols rather than purely clinician-led, ad hoc formats (Jang et al., 2023). A systematic review and meta-analysis of telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal disorders reported comparable effectiveness and favourable cost-effectiveness against conventional in-person care (Molina-Garcia et al., 2024), and a broader umbrella and meta-meta-analysis review of telerehabilitation across physical therapy practice found consistent evidence of effectiveness across a wide range of conditions (Suso-Martí et al., 2021). None of these studies are about strength or power testing specifically, but they make the same underlying point: when care is delivered through a structured, repeatable protocol rather than being reinvented by each clinician in the moment, outcomes hold up, and in some cases the cost profile improves too.

Standardised testing is the assessment-side equivalent of that argument. If a structured protocol can replace clinician-to-clinician variation in delivery without a drop in outcomes, there's no reason to assume clinician-to-clinician variation in measurement is somehow protective. It's the opposite: it's the part of the pathway most exposed to unwarranted disagreement, because unlike treatment technique, a strength or range of movement number should not depend on who's holding the tape measure.

What this looks like in a normal working week

Reducing variation doesn't require a system overhaul. It requires a small set of decisions applied consistently.

  • Fix the protocol before the number matters. Position, warm-up, number of reps, and testing order need to be identical whoever is running the session, or the number itself becomes noise disguised as data.
  • Score against normative expectation, not a colleague's opinion. A percentage of expected score for age, sex and weight removes the need for two clinicians to agree on a subjective grade before they can agree on a plan.
  • Use the same two PROMs across the team. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale and a three-dimensional pain score give every clinician the same functional and symptom baseline to work from, regardless of who did the initial assessment.
  • Flag deviation from expected trajectory as the trigger for discussion, not a clinician's gut feeling that something looks off. That turns case discussions into data conversations rather than opinion exchanges.
  • Audit variation directly. Once a year, pull the same test across every clinician's caseload and look at whether outcomes cluster by patient presentation or by which clinician did the assessment. If it's the latter, that's the variation worth addressing first.

None of this removes the clinical reasoning that makes physiotherapy a skilled profession. It removes the part of the variation that was never really clinical reasoning in the first place, it was just measurement drift dressed up as clinical judgement.

Where to start

You don't need to standardise everything a clinic does to see the benefit. Pick one condition your team sees often, agree a fixed test battery for it across every clinician, and run it for a month. Compare the spread in results not by patient, but by clinician. Benchmark PS is built to make that comparison straightforward, because every strength, endurance, range of movement and power test is captured the same way and scored against the same normative reference, whoever is running the session. The goal isn't to replace clinical judgement with a number. It's to make sure that when ten clinicians look at the same patient, they're starting from the same page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does standardised testing replace clinical judgement?

No. It gives clinical judgement a consistent reference point. Objective, normative-comparison data narrows the disagreements that come from subjective grading and inconsistent test protocols, but the decision about what to do with that data still sits with the clinician.

Why does clinician-to-clinician variation matter more in direct access services?

When patients reach a physiotherapist without a referral gatekeeping their initial assessment, that first assessment carries the whole episode of care. If ten clinicians assess the same presentation ten different ways, there's no second check to catch an inconsistent judgement call.

How does Benchmark PS standardise strength testing across a team?

Strength is captured using handheld dynamometry, benchmarked against peer-reviewed normative data, or gym-based lifts benchmarked against Strengthlevel.com data, and expressed as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight, regardless of which clinician runs the test.

Where should a clinic start if it wants to reduce variation between clinicians?

Pick one condition seen often, agree a single fixed test battery for it across the whole team, and run it for a month. Compare results by clinician rather than by patient to see where the inconsistency actually sits before trying to standardise everything at once.
