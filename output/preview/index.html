Shoulder PROMs in Athletes: Which Outcome Measures Actually Hold Up

Shoulder shoulder PROMs outcome measures sports physiotherapy

Why generic shoulder questionnaires struggle in athletic populations, what the reliability evidence actually shows, and how to build a measurement protocol that holds up.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Ask ten physiotherapists which shoulder outcome measure they trust for an overhead athlete and you'll get ten different answers, usually followed by a caveat. That caveat is worth taking seriously. Most of the questionnaires clinicians reach for in shoulder rehab were built and validated in populations that look nothing like a county-level swimmer, a club cricketer, or a semi-professional thrower returning from a rotator cuff problem. Understanding where these tools hold up and where they don't changes what you should actually be measuring in clinic.

The evidence base wasn't built with athletes in mind

The clinical practice guidelines that underpin most shoulder rehabilitation decisions were synthesised from trials in general orthopaedic and occupational shoulder pain populations, not competitive athletes managing sport-specific load (Philadelphia, 2001). That matters because the functional demands, baseline capacity, and recovery expectations of a 34-year-old office worker with subacromial pain and a 19-year-old overhead thrower are not comparable, yet the outcome tools applied to both are frequently the same.

The problem compounds when you look across the trial literature for a single shoulder condition. Reviews assessing therapeutic trials for conditions like adhesive capsulitis routinely find inconsistent selection and reporting of outcome measures between studies (Fares et al., 2023). If the trials underpinning your evidence base can't agree on what to measure, comparing your own patient's trajectory against a published benchmark is shakier ground than it looks on paper.

Reliability problems show up before validity ones

Before asking whether a shoulder outcome measure captures the right construct, ask whether it produces the same answer twice. This is where a lot of routine shoulder assessment quietly breaks down. Systematic review evidence on specific physical examination tests for shoulder pathology shows wide variation in diagnostic reliability depending on the test, the rater, and the protocol used (Lange et al., 2017). Even measurement approaches that look objective on the surface, such as acromiohumeral distance measurement in rotator cuff tendinopathy, lack a single reliable, clinically applicable method that's been consistently validated for routine use (McCreesh et al., 2015).

The practical takeaway is uncomfortable but important: a shoulder outcome is only as defensible as the measurement process sitting underneath it. If two clinicians in the same clinic can't reproduce each other's findings on the same patient, the PROM or performance score attached to that assessment inherits the same instability, regardless of how well-validated the questionnaire itself is.

  • Standardise positioning, instruction, and scoring criteria before you trust a shoulder measure across sessions
  • Treat any single-session physical exam finding as provisional until it's reproduced
  • Prioritise measures with published reliability data specific to the population you're testing, not just validity data from a different cohort

Performance-based functional tests close a gap questionnaires leave open

Self-report questionnaires tell you how a patient feels about their shoulder. They don't tell you what the shoulder can actually do under load. That distinction matters most in athletes, where the return-to-sport decision hinges on capacity, not comfort. A systematic review and meta-analysis of performance-based functional tests in shoulder pathology found these measures provide psychometric information that self-report tools don't capture on their own, supporting their use as a complement to, not a replacement for, questionnaire-based outcomes (Nazari et al., 2020).

This is echoed in return-to-sport specific testing. A shoulder and arm return-to-sport test battery designed for athletic populations has shown acceptable reliability across its component tests, which means it can be trusted to track genuine change rather than measurement noise as an athlete progresses through rehab (Olds et al., 2019). For time-pressed clinicians, the message is straightforward: pair a self-report score with a performance-based capacity test, and don't rely on either one in isolation to clear an athlete for return to sport.

Why a generic questionnaire score misses what matters to the athlete in front of you

Generic shoulder scales are built around items that apply to the average shoulder pain patient: reaching overhead, sleeping on the affected side, lifting light objects. An athlete whose problem only appears at end-range external rotation under high angular velocity, or at the top of a throwing motion, can score close to the ceiling on a generic scale while still being nowhere near ready to compete. The scale isn't wrong, it's just answering the wrong question for that patient.

This is why an individualised approach to patient-reported function performs better for athletic populations than a fixed generic item list. Asking a patient to identify and rate their own specific functional limitations, rather than scoring them against a standard set of everyday tasks, captures the demand that actually matters to them. Pain also needs more nuance than a single 0-10 rating at rest. Frequency, intensity, and duration behave differently across a rehab timeline, and averaging the three gives a more complete picture of where an athlete's pain experience is actually heading, rather than a single number that can mask whether pain is becoming less frequent, less intense, or shorter-lived.

Building a shoulder assessment protocol that holds up under scrutiny

None of this means throwing out PROMs. It means being deliberate about which ones you use and pairing them with objective performance data that's reliable enough to trust session to session. A defensible shoulder protocol for an athletic caseload looks something like this:

  1. Patient-specific function. Let the athlete identify their own limiting activities rather than scoring them against a generic item bank.
  2. Three-dimensional pain tracking. Capture frequency, intensity, and duration separately rather than a single resting pain score.
  3. Range of movement. Track shoulder range with any inclinometer tool, measured the same way every session.
  4. Strength. Use handheld dynamometer data benchmarked against peer-reviewed normative literature, expressed as a percentage of expected score for age, sex, and weight, rather than a raw number with no context.
  5. Power. For throwing athletes, a medicine ball throw distance measured with a tape measure gives a low-cost, technology-agnostic read on upper-limb power output that a questionnaire simply can't provide.
  6. Longitudinal tracking. Plot PROM and performance data against each other over time. A rising PSFS score alongside a flat power output is a different clinical picture than both moving together.

The common thread across the reliability and psychometric literature is that measurement quality, not the sophistication of the tool, is what determines whether an outcome measure holds up. A simple test performed the same way every time beats a validated questionnaire applied inconsistently.

Making the shift from questionnaire-only to measured practice

Most clinics already collect some form of shoulder PROM. Far fewer pair it consistently with reliable, standardised performance data, which is exactly the gap the evidence above points to. Benchmark PS was built to close that gap without requiring expensive equipment: patient-specific function and three-dimensional pain scoring alongside range of movement, strength, and power testing, all tracked longitudinally against normative expectation. If your shoulder caseload includes athletes returning to sport, that combination gives you a far more defensible basis for the return-to-play decision than a questionnaire score on its own.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why don't generic shoulder questionnaires work well for athletes?

Most generic shoulder PROMs were validated in general orthopaedic or occupational populations, not competitive athletes. Their item sets focus on everyday tasks like reaching or sleeping, so athletic patients can score near the ceiling while still lacking the capacity to return to sport-specific demands.

Should I stop using patient-reported outcome measures for shoulder athletes?

No. The evidence supports pairing self-report measures with performance-based functional testing rather than replacing one with the other. Self-report and performance data capture different information, and using both gives a more complete and defensible picture of readiness.

What matters more for shoulder PROMs, validity or reliability?

Reliability comes first in practice. If two clinicians can't reproduce the same finding on the same patient, the outcome measure attached to that assessment is unstable regardless of how well the tool was validated in research settings. Standardised, repeatable testing protocols should be the starting point.

Continue Conservative Care or Refer On: The Decision Points in Lumbar Radiculopathy

Clinical Decision-Making lumbar radiculopathy conservative care referral criteria low back pain

Lumbar radiculopathy referral decisions shouldn't hinge on gut feel. Here are the objective checkpoints that tell you when conservative care is working.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Most lumbar radiculopathy resolves with conservative management, which is exactly what makes the referral decision hard rather than easy. If every case needed surgery, or every case settled with rest and reassurance, there would be no decision to make. The reality sits in between: a meaningful minority of patients don't improve on the timeline conservative care assumes, and the clinician managing them week to week is usually the one left to notice when the trajectory has stalled. In practice that decision often gets made on gut feel, a bad week for the patient, a difficult conversation, rather than on anything that was tracked from the first appointment.

The problem isn't clinical judgement. It's that judgement without a baseline has nothing to compare against. If you didn't record how much leg pain limited function in week one, "not really improving" in week six is an impression, not a finding. Treating radiculopathy conservatively for six, eight, twelve weeks without a structured re-assessment point is common practice, but it's also why referral decisions get made later than they should, after the patient has lost confidence in the plan rather than because the data crossed a threshold.

Ruling in radiculopathy, and ruling out its mimics

Before any conservative trial has a defined endpoint, the diagnosis needs to be right, and radicular leg pain has more mimics than most clinicians give it credit for. Lumbar spinal stenosis presents with leg pain and neurogenic claudication that can look radicular on a bad day, but its natural history and referral thresholds are different enough that lumping it in with disc-related radiculopathy skews the conservative trial from the outset (Lurie et al., 2016). A patient with genuine stenosis who fails a structured trial of activity modification and loading has a different surgical calculus to a 35-year-old with an acute disc protrusion, and running both on the same clock does neither any favours.

Sacroiliac joint pain is the other common confusion. Posterior thigh and buttock referral from the SIJ is frequently read as radicular, particularly when a formal neurological exam wasn't repeated to confirm a genuine dermatomal pattern, and current guidance on SIJ pain management is clear that it needs its own diagnostic pathway and its own conservative benchmarks, not radiculopathy's (Migliorini et al., 2025). Getting this differentiation right in the first or second appointment matters more than any decision point that comes later, because a conservative trial measured against the wrong diagnosis will never produce a clean answer either way.

What a defensible conservative trial actually contains

A conservative trial that holds up, to the patient, to a referring GP, to whoever reviews the case at six weeks, needs three things: a technique with some evidence behind it, a baseline, and a re-test schedule. Neural mobilisation has a reasonable evidence base for low back and radicular pain, with systematic review data showing benefit for pain and function when added to standard management, which makes it a legitimate part of the trial rather than something added to fill the session (Peacock et al., 2023). What most trials are missing isn't the technique. It's the numbers on either side of it.

At minimum that means capturing the patient's own functional limitations against a baseline, using a tool such as the Patient-Specific Functional Scale, alongside a structured pain measure that captures frequency, intensity and duration rather than a single 0-10 number that moves with the patient's mood on the day. Range of movement, taken with an inclinometer, and strength through the key lower limb muscle groups affected by the suspected nerve root, ankle dorsiflexion, great toe extension, plantarflexion, quadriceps, tested against normative data for the patient's age, sex and weight rather than eyeballed as "4 out of 5", turn a vague impression of progress into a number you can track against an expected rate of change. None of this needs to happen twice a week. It needs to happen at intake and again at a defined re-test point, so week six is being compared to week one rather than to how the clinician remembers week one.

The checkpoints that tell you to refer

Red flags aside, cauda equina symptoms, progressive motor deficit, and unexplained systemic signs are an immediate referral regardless of where a patient sits in a conservative programme, the decision to refer for imaging or a surgical opinion in uncomplicated radiculopathy usually comes down to a handful of measurable checkpoints rather than a single dramatic event:

  • No meaningful movement in PSFS or pain score by the defined re-test point. If the change since baseline sits below the expected minimal clinically important difference at six weeks, that's data telling you the current plan isn't working, not a reason to extend it unchanged.
  • Strength against normative expectation is flat or declining. A patient whose myotomal strength percentage hasn't moved, or has dropped, between intake and re-test is a different clinical picture from one who's slowly closing the gap, even if both report similar pain scores.
  • Progressive neurological signs. Any new or worsening motor deficit between visits moves the conversation from continue and reassess to refer, regardless of how the pain score reads.
  • Activity level dropping below recommended thresholds. A patient becoming progressively less active against NHS guideline levels, driven by fear or avoidance rather than genuine incapacity, is a marker that the conservative approach is losing the patient's buy-in, which matters for the referral conversation even before it matters for the biology.

None of these checkpoints require a dramatic escalation to apply. They require being checked on a schedule that was set before the trial started, rather than whenever the patient's frustration forces the conversation.

This decision architecture isn't unique to the low back

The same structure, conservative first, with a defined trial period and objective checkpoints rather than an open-ended wait, shows up across MSK conditions where surgery is an option but not a default. Meta-analysis evidence comparing surgical and conservative management of rotator cuff tears consistently finds outcomes converge over time in appropriately selected patients, which is exactly why the decision hinges on defined checkpoints during the conservative period rather than a blanket rule applied at presentation (Longo et al., 2021) (Ryösä et al., 2017). The ProFHER trial on proximal humerus fractures found broadly comparable outcomes between surgical and non-surgical management, reinforcing that operate-or-don't is rarely decided at the first appointment, it's decided by how the conservative pathway performs against pre-set markers (Handoll et al., 2015).

The lesson for radiculopathy isn't that these conditions behave the same way clinically, they don't. It's that the decision-making architecture, baseline, trial period, objective re-test, defined threshold for escalation, is the same regardless of joint or tissue. Clinicians who build that structure once tend to apply it consistently across a caseload rather than reinventing the referral conversation for every patient.

Building the checkpoint into your caseload

None of this requires new imaging pathways or a change to referral criteria your MDT already agrees on. It requires capturing a baseline on the first visit and setting a re-test date before the trial starts, so the decision to continue or refer is made against numbers rather than against how the last consultation felt. Benchmark PS is built to make that baseline and re-test cheap to capture in a normal appointment, PSFS, pain score, inclinometer range of movement, and handheld dynamometer strength against normative data, tracked longitudinally so the six-week checkpoint is a comparison rather than a guess. If your current radiculopathy pathway doesn't have a re-test date on the calendar from the first appointment, that's the gap worth closing before the next patient walks in.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a conservative trial for lumbar radiculopathy run before considering referral?

There's no universal number, but six weeks is a common checkpoint in practice, provided a baseline was captured at intake using tools like the PSFS, pain score, and strength testing against normative data, so the re-test is a real comparison rather than a subjective impression.

What's the difference between lumbar radiculopathy and lumbar spinal stenosis in a conservative care setting?

Both can produce leg pain, but stenosis is associated with neurogenic claudication and a different natural history and referral threshold, so treating it on the same conservative timeline as disc-related radiculopathy risks under- or over-treating one of the two conditions.

Which objective markers matter most when deciding whether to refer a radiculopathy patient?

PSFS and pain score movement against the minimal clinically important difference, strength against normative expectation for the relevant myotomes, any progressive neurological signs, and activity level relative to NHS guidelines are the checkpoints that carry the most weight.

Power Testing Without a Force Plate: What the Drop Jump Can Tell You in Clinic

Objective Testing Protocols power testing drop jump ankle rehabilitation Achilles rehabilitation

Drop jump testing gives you objective power and landing data without a force plate. Here's how to run it, what it tells you, and where it changes management.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Ask a physiotherapist to test knee extension strength and most will reach for a handheld dynamometer without thinking twice. Ask the same clinician to test lower limb power and the conversation usually stalls at "we don't have a force plate." Power gets left out of the assessment entirely, or it gets reduced to a subjective impression of how a patient looks when they jump. Neither is good enough for a domain that sits directly on the pathway back to running, cutting, and field sport.

The drop jump is one of the most useful tools you already have access to, and it doesn't need a force plate to be useful. What it needs is a consistent protocol, a way to capture ground contact time and jump height, and a normative framework to judge the result against. All three are achievable with equipment most clinics already own.

The power gap in most physiotherapy assessments

Range of movement gets measured with a goniometer or inclinometer. Strength, when it's tested at all, usually gets a manual muscle test score rather than a number. Power tends to fall off the bottom of the list altogether, not because it matters less clinically, but because clinicians associate it with lab equipment they don't have and don't have budget for.

That's a gap with consequences. Reactive strength, the ability to absorb load and reproduce force quickly through the stretch-shortening cycle, is exactly what's being asked of a knee, ankle, or Achilles tendon the moment a patient returns to running, jumping, or changing direction. Testing strength in isolation and assuming power will follow is an assumption, not a measurement.

What a drop jump actually tells you

The protocol itself is simple. The patient steps off a box of a fixed height, typically 20 to 40cm, lands, and immediately rebounds into a maximal vertical jump with minimal ground contact time. Two numbers come out of it: how long the foot was in contact with the floor, and how high the rebound jump was. Divide jump height by contact time and you get a reactive strength index, a proxy for how efficiently someone converts landing force into upward propulsion.

None of that requires a force plate. A slow-motion video on a smartphone, timed through an app such as MyJump2, gives contact time and flight time to a workable degree of accuracy from ordinary clinic footage. A tape measure against a wall marks jump height for a standing vertical jump variant if you want something even simpler. Force plates and jump mats can generate the same numbers with more precision, but for the vast majority of MSK caseloads the smartphone version is accurate enough to track change over time and flag patients who are meaningfully below where they should be.

What matters clinically is not chasing lab-grade precision, it's applying the same protocol every time, on the same equipment, so this month's number and last month's number are actually comparable.

Where it changes management: ankle instability and Achilles rehab

Landing mechanics and reactive strength are not academic curiosities in ankle and Achilles caseloads, they're central to how these patients fail and how they're rehabilitated. A randomised controlled trial of hop-stabilisation training in athletes with chronic ankle instability found that structured landing-focused training changed landing biomechanics and improved outcome measures compared with standard care (Ardakani et al., 2019). If landing quality and reactive capacity respond to targeted training, they need to be assessed in the first place, not inferred from a strength test alone.

A 2025 network meta-analysis comparing physiotherapy approaches for chronic ankle instability found balance and strength-based interventions delivered the strongest effects among the options studied (Yang et al., 2025), and earlier RCT work has shown that strength-training protocols meaningfully reduce the deficits seen in this population (Hall et al., 2015). Power sits downstream of both balance and strength, so a patient who tests well on strength but hasn't been retested on a landing task is only half assessed.

The same logic applies after acute ankle sprain. Accelerated, functionally-loaded rehabilitation has been shown to outperform standard protect-and-rest management on recovery of function (Bleakley et al., 2010), and a scoping review of rehabilitation evidence after lateral ankle sprain in athletes points to progressive functional loading, not time on the calendar, as the driver of readiness to return (Tee et al., 2022). Drop jump testing gives you an objective marker of that functional loading tolerance rather than a guess based on how many weeks have passed.

Achilles tendon rehabilitation tells a similar story. A systematic review of early functional rehabilitation after acute Achilles tendon rupture identified progressive loading and functional milestones, rather than fixed timeframes, as the components associated with better outcomes (Zellers et al., 2019). A tendon that has to tolerate rapid stretch-shortening load on return to sport needs to be tested under conditions that resemble that demand, and a controlled drop jump is the closest most clinics can get to that without a running track.

Building a low-tech drop jump protocol into a 30-minute slot

You don't need a dedicated power-testing session to make this routine. A basic protocol takes under five minutes once the patient is warmed up:

  • Use a fixed box height for every session with that patient, and record what it was.
  • Film from the side at a consistent distance, using slow-motion or a contact-time app.
  • Run three trials and keep the best, discarding any where the patient visibly steps rather than falls off the box.
  • Record contact time and rebound jump height separately, not just the combined index, so you can see which one is driving a poor score.
  • Repeat at the same time of session (post-warm-up, pre-fatigue) at every review point.

If a patient can't safely tolerate a drop jump yet, a countermovement jump measured with a tape measure against a wall, or a single-leg hop for distance, gives you a lower-demand power marker to track while loading capacity builds.

Interpreting the numbers without an arbitrary cut-off

The temptation with any jump-based test is to reach for a symmetry comparison, left versus right, and set a pass mark at some round number. Resist it. A fixed symmetry threshold ignores that the uninjured side can itself be detrained, and it tells you nothing about whether either limb is actually performing where it should for that patient's age, sex, and training background.

A more defensible approach is to compare the result against what would be expected for that patient's profile and track the percentage change over successive sessions. A patient scoring well below their expected range on reactive strength, even with a strength test that looks adequate, is telling you something the strength number alone missed. That's the value of testing power as its own domain rather than assuming it tracks strength one-to-one.

Longitudinal tracking matters as much as the single-session number. A drop jump score means little in isolation, it becomes clinically useful once you can show a trend line moving toward the expected range, or flag that it's plateaued while strength keeps improving, which points to a different rehab emphasis.

Benchmark's power domain works with the tools already sitting in most clinics, smartphone jump apps, medicine balls, and tape measures, and expresses results as a percentage of the patient's expected score based on age, sex, and weight, with the trend tracked alongside your strength and range of movement data over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you test power reliably without a force plate?

Yes. Smartphone apps such as MyJump2 can calculate contact time and jump height from video with accuracy sufficient for tracking clinical change, and a tape measure works for simpler vertical jump variants. Precision instrumentation isn't necessary for most MSK caseloads, consistency of protocol is what matters.

What does the drop jump measure that a strength test doesn't?

A drop jump captures reactive strength, the ability to absorb landing force and redirect it into upward propulsion quickly. This stretch-shortening cycle capacity doesn't automatically follow from isolated strength gains, so it needs its own test to catch deficits that strength testing alone would miss.

Is drop jump testing appropriate for ankle sprain and Achilles rehab patients?

It's highly relevant once a patient has progressed enough to tolerate the loading, since both conditions require the tendon or joint to cope with rapid stretch-shortening demand on return to sport. Evidence supports progressive functional loading over fixed timeframes as the better predictor of readiness, and a controlled drop jump gives you an objective functional loading marker rather than a calendar-based guess.

Should I compare left and right drop jump scores?

A fixed symmetry threshold isn't a reliable standard, since the uninjured limb can itself be undertrained. Comparing each limb's result to a normative expectation for that patient's age, sex, and weight, and tracking the trend over time, gives a more defensible picture than a left-right ratio alone.

Reducing Treatment Variation Between Clinicians: What Standardised Testing Changes

Objective Assessment treatment variation standardised testing objective assessment clinical consistency

Why identical patients get different care from different physiotherapists, and how standardised testing closes that gap without removing clinical judgement.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Two physiotherapists see the same patient with the same knee pain in the same week, and there's a fair chance they run different tests, apply different cut-offs, and reach different conclusions about what counts as ready. That's not a hypothetical. It's the default state of most MSK services, because strength, range of movement and functional capacity are still judged largely by feel. One clinician's grade 4 out of 5 on manual muscle testing is another's grade 4+. One decides a patient can progress load based on how the session felt; another wants a specific number under load before agreeing. None of this reflects a lack of skill. It reflects the absence of a shared, objective reference point that every clinician in the building is working from.

Standardised testing doesn't take clinical judgement out of the room. It gives that judgement something consistent to sit on. When every clinician in a service measures the same variable, the same way, against the same normative reference, the variation that remains is the variation that should exist: different patients, different presentations, different goals. The variation that shouldn't exist, two clinicians reaching different conclusions from identical information, gets squeezed out.

Where clinician-to-clinician variation actually comes from

Most variation between clinicians doesn't come from disagreement about diagnosis. It comes from the measurement step in between diagnosis and decision. Manual muscle testing grades are ordinal and rater-dependent. Goniometry readings drift between examiners by several degrees even with training. Functional criteria like "ready to progress" or "safe to load" are frequently a clinical impression rather than a number, which means the same patient can get a green light from one clinician and a hold from another, on the same day, with no change in presentation.

This matters more in services built around clinician autonomy at first contact. A systematic review of direct access physiotherapy, where patients reach a physiotherapist without a prior referral or gatekeeping consultation, found outcomes hold up well when clinicians exercise full clinical judgement from the first appointment (Gallotti et al., 2023). That's a genuinely positive finding for the profession, but it also raises the stakes on consistency. If there's no second clinician checking the reasoning, the quality of the initial assessment carries the whole episode of care. A service where ten clinicians assess the same presentation ten different ways isn't protected by autonomy, it's exposed by it.

None of this is really about individual competence. It's a structural gap: most physiotherapy training teaches manual assessment technique thoroughly and objective benchmarking barely at all, so clinicians default to whatever heuristics they picked up in their first few years of practice. Multiply that across a team of eight or ten clinicians trained in different rotations, under different supervisors, and the spread in judgement calls becomes wide enough that two patients with identical presentations can end up on measurably different care pathways.

What changes when the test itself is standardised

The fix isn't a new test. It's the same tests, run the same way, scored against the same reference every time, regardless of which clinician is in the room. Range of movement captured with a consistent inclinometer protocol. Endurance assessed with a fixed timed-hold or max-rep format. Strength captured either through handheld dynamometry, benchmarked against peer-reviewed normative data, or through gym-based lifts benchmarked against Strengthlevel.com population data, expressed as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight. Power assessed through jump or throw tests using nothing more exotic than a smartphone app, a medicine ball or a tape measure.

The output that matters here isn't the raw number. It's the percentage of normative expectation. A clinician reading "68% of expected strength for age and sex" doesn't need to argue with a colleague about whether that's a grade 4 or a grade 4+. The number does the work that used to depend on two people agreeing on a subjective scale. Paired with the Patient-Specific Functional Scale and a three-dimensional pain score covering frequency, intensity and duration, the picture a clinician is working from stops being "what did this feel like in the room" and starts being "where does this patient sit against an expected trajectory, and how has that shifted since the last visit."

That shift is what actually reduces variation. It's not that the objective number is infallible, it's that every clinician in the service is reading the same number, generated the same way, so disagreements move from "what grade is this" to "given this data, what should we do about it", which is a far more productive conversation and one that's easier to audit.

The evidence base itself hasn't settled on one protocol either

Clinician variation isn't purely a training or discipline problem. It's partly downstream of an evidence base that hasn't converged on single protocols either, which leaves clinicians choosing between genuinely competing, evidence-supported approaches. A trial comparing two well-established eccentric loading protocols for chronic Achilles tendinopathy head to head found both are defensible options with different loading structures, rather than one clearly superior regime (Habets et al., 2017). When even the trial literature offers two valid roads, individual clinicians default to whichever one they trained under, and that choice alone creates variation in what "standard treatment" looks like across a team.

The same pattern shows up at scale. A synthesis of 55 randomised controlled trials covering more than 4,300 patients receiving physical therapy after lumbar disc herniation surgery had to pool a genuinely heterogeneous set of rehabilitation protocols to draw any conclusions at all (Brotis et al., 2025). A systematic review of physiotherapy after lumbar fusion surgery found a similarly fragmented picture, with wide differences in timing, intensity and content of rehabilitation programmes across the included studies, and correspondingly limited ability to say which specific protocol elements were driving outcomes (Madera et al., 2017).

That heterogeneity in the published evidence doesn't excuse variation in the clinic, but it does explain where a chunk of it comes from. If the research hasn't settled the protocol question, clinicians are left making individual judgement calls about programme design on top of individual judgement calls about assessment. Standardised testing can't resolve the first problem, the field will keep debating loading protocols for years. What it can do is stop the second problem, inconsistent measurement, from compounding the first.

Consistency changes decisions, not just documentation

The case for standardisation isn't purely about tidier notes. It's about whether the treatment decision made on the back of a test is the same decision a colleague would have made from the same data. There's a reasonable evidence base showing that structured, software-supported delivery models hold their own against traditional face-to-face judgement calls, which is a useful proof of concept for the same principle applied to assessment.

A meta-analysis of digital healthcare-supported rehabilitation across musculoskeletal conditions found meaningful improvements in pain and quality-of-life outcomes when care was delivered through structured digital protocols rather than purely clinician-led, ad hoc formats (Jang et al., 2023). A systematic review and meta-analysis of telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal disorders reported comparable effectiveness and favourable cost-effectiveness against conventional in-person care (Molina-Garcia et al., 2024), and a broader umbrella and meta-meta-analysis review of telerehabilitation across physical therapy practice found consistent evidence of effectiveness across a wide range of conditions (Suso-Martí et al., 2021). None of these studies are about strength or power testing specifically, but they make the same underlying point: when care is delivered through a structured, repeatable protocol rather than being reinvented by each clinician in the moment, outcomes hold up, and in some cases the cost profile improves too.

Standardised testing is the assessment-side equivalent of that argument. If a structured protocol can replace clinician-to-clinician variation in delivery without a drop in outcomes, there's no reason to assume clinician-to-clinician variation in measurement is somehow protective. It's the opposite: it's the part of the pathway most exposed to unwarranted disagreement, because unlike treatment technique, a strength or range of movement number should not depend on who's holding the tape measure.

What this looks like in a normal working week

Reducing variation doesn't require a system overhaul. It requires a small set of decisions applied consistently.

  • Fix the protocol before the number matters. Position, warm-up, number of reps, and testing order need to be identical whoever is running the session, or the number itself becomes noise disguised as data.
  • Score against normative expectation, not a colleague's opinion. A percentage of expected score for age, sex and weight removes the need for two clinicians to agree on a subjective grade before they can agree on a plan.
  • Use the same two PROMs across the team. The Patient-Specific Functional Scale and a three-dimensional pain score give every clinician the same functional and symptom baseline to work from, regardless of who did the initial assessment.
  • Flag deviation from expected trajectory as the trigger for discussion, not a clinician's gut feeling that something looks off. That turns case discussions into data conversations rather than opinion exchanges.
  • Audit variation directly. Once a year, pull the same test across every clinician's caseload and look at whether outcomes cluster by patient presentation or by which clinician did the assessment. If it's the latter, that's the variation worth addressing first.

None of this removes the clinical reasoning that makes physiotherapy a skilled profession. It removes the part of the variation that was never really clinical reasoning in the first place, it was just measurement drift dressed up as clinical judgement.

Where to start

You don't need to standardise everything a clinic does to see the benefit. Pick one condition your team sees often, agree a fixed test battery for it across every clinician, and run it for a month. Compare the spread in results not by patient, but by clinician. Benchmark PS is built to make that comparison straightforward, because every strength, endurance, range of movement and power test is captured the same way and scored against the same normative reference, whoever is running the session. The goal isn't to replace clinical judgement with a number. It's to make sure that when ten clinicians look at the same patient, they're starting from the same page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does standardised testing replace clinical judgement?

No. It gives clinical judgement a consistent reference point. Objective, normative-comparison data narrows the disagreements that come from subjective grading and inconsistent test protocols, but the decision about what to do with that data still sits with the clinician.

Why does clinician-to-clinician variation matter more in direct access services?

When patients reach a physiotherapist without a referral gatekeeping their initial assessment, that first assessment carries the whole episode of care. If ten clinicians assess the same presentation ten different ways, there's no second check to catch an inconsistent judgement call.

How does Benchmark PS standardise strength testing across a team?

Strength is captured using handheld dynamometry, benchmarked against peer-reviewed normative data, or gym-based lifts benchmarked against Strengthlevel.com data, and expressed as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight, regardless of which clinician runs the test.

Where should a clinic start if it wants to reduce variation between clinicians?

Pick one condition seen often, agree a single fixed test battery for it across the whole team, and run it for a month. Compare results by clinician rather than by patient to see where the inconsistency actually sits before trying to standardise everything at once.
