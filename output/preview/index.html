Shoulder PROMs in Athletes: Which Outcome Measures Actually Hold Up

Shoulder and Upper Limb shoulder PROMs PSFS outcome measures

Which shoulder outcome measures stay useful in athletes? A clinical look at ceiling effects, interpretation, and pairing PSFS and pain scores with objective testing.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Most shoulder outcome measures were built for people with shoulder pain who struggle to dress, sleep or reach a shelf. Your overhead athlete is usually someone else. They are back at work, back in training, and still unable to throw at full effort or serve without guarding. The questionnaire says they are fine. The athlete knows they are not.

This article looks at what a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) has to do to be useful in an athletic shoulder, where the usual tools tend to fail, and how to build a measurement set that holds up when the stakes are return to sport.

What a PROM has to do in an athlete

A PROM earns its place in clinic if it does three things. It has to detect a change that matters to the patient. It has to be interpretable, meaning you know what a given score change represents beyond measurement noise. And it has to be relevant to the activities the patient cares about.

Athletes challenge all three. Their baseline function is high, so a general shoulder questionnaire can hit its ceiling while the patient still cannot perform their sport. Their goals are specific, so a fixed list of items about daily tasks misses the demands that matter. And their symptoms are often intermittent, appearing only under high load.

Research on functional activity in people with shoulder dysfunction shows that the activities that provoke difficulty vary between individuals and between conditions (Lin et al., 2005). That is an argument for measures that let the patient name the activity, rather than assuming it from a fixed item list.

Why the patient-nominated approach holds up

The Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) asks the patient to identify their own functional limitations and rate each on a 0 to 10 scale. For an athlete, those activities might be a first serve, a bowling spell, a snatch or a butterfly stroke. The tool adapts to the person rather than the other way round.

That has two practical advantages. First, ceiling effects matter less, because the patient is scoring the exact task they cannot yet do. Second, the score maps directly onto the goal you are rehabilitating towards, which makes the conversation about progress far easier.

The limitation is comparability. Two athletes will nominate different tasks, so PSFS does not let you compare across patients in the way a fixed instrument would. Its strength is within-patient change over time, which is where most clinical decisions are made anyway.

Practical points when using it with athletes:

  • Ask for sport-specific tasks, not general ones. "Overhead reach" is weaker than "cocking phase of the throw at full effort".
  • Record the same tasks at every review. Changing the activities mid-episode removes the value of the trend.
  • Re-score at every visit, not only at baseline and discharge. Trajectory tells you more than the end points.

Pain: one number is not enough

A single pain rating at rest is a poor guide in an athlete. Pain may be absent in the clinic and present only after thirty throws. A single score also hides whether pain is occasional or constant, brief or lingering.

The three-dimensional pain score takes the mean of pain frequency, intensity and duration. This separates a shoulder that hurts sharply once a week from one that aches most of the day at a lower intensity, which a single rating would score the same. In practice, a falling frequency with unchanged intensity is a different clinical picture from the reverse, and it points to different decisions about load.

Interpretation needs context. Benchmark presents the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and the expected rate of change alongside PROM scores. That matters because a two-point move on a 0 to 10 scale means little without knowing whether it exceeds the measurement error and whether it is faster or slower than expected for that patient. Without that reference, clinicians tend to read what they hope to see.

The reliability question: what to trust and what to treat with caution

Reliability is not a property of a tool in general. It depends on the population, the tester and the way the measure is used. Work on classification of shoulder pain found that agreement between testers depends heavily on the system used and the training of the examiner (Carter et al., 2012). If a subjective label can shift between clinicians, a patient-reported number and a standardised physical test give you a steadier anchor.

The same applies to questionnaires borrowed from other settings. Psychometric work on questionnaires in patients with shoulder pain shows that how well an instrument performs has to be tested in the shoulder population, not assumed from its performance elsewhere (Mintken et al., 2010). Before adding any new scale to your routine, ask what evidence exists in athletic shoulders specifically. In most cases it is thin. That is a reason to keep your PROM set small and to pair it with physical measures, rather than adding more questionnaires.

A similar lesson applies to physical measures. Reliability work on a shoulder and arm return-to-sport test battery shows that standardised protocols can be repeated consistently when the procedure is fixed (Olds et al., 2019). The detail that carries over to clinic is the discipline: same positions, same instructions, same number of trials, every time.

Pairing PROMs with objective data

The most important point is that no PROM should be the only evidence for a return-to-sport decision. Patient-reported function tells you what the athlete believes and experiences. Objective testing tells you what the shoulder can do. When they disagree, that disagreement is the most useful clinical finding you have.

Benchmark covers four physical domains: range of movement from any inclinometer, endurance from timed holds or maximum repetitions, strength from handheld dynamometry or gym-based lifts, and power from jumps and throws using a smartphone app, medicine ball or tape measure. Results are expressed as a percentage of the expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight, so you are comparing against a normative expectation rather than guessing.

Consider three common patterns:

  1. High PSFS, low percentage of expected strength. The athlete feels ready but the shoulder is under-capacity. Hold progression and load the deficit.
  2. Low PSFS, strength at or above expected. Capacity is not the limiting factor. Look at load exposure, technique demands of the sport, and what the patient is actually avoiding.
  3. Both improving together. The picture is coherent and the case for progression is defensible.

Range of movement deserves the same standardisation. Studies of scapular upward rotation and posture measures in shoulder patients show that the method of measurement changes what you can conclude (Johnson et al., 2001). Use one method and one set-up for the episode so that change reflects the patient, not the technique.

What the evidence base can and cannot tell you

Be honest about the limits. Guidelines on rehabilitation for shoulder pain have long noted that evidence for many interventions is limited in quality (Philadelphia, 2001), and studies of rehabilitation in athletes such as tennis players tend to be small (Ma et al., 2022). Neither gives you a validated athletic shoulder PROM threshold for return to sport.

That is why a trend against an expected rate of change, supported by objective data, is a more defensible basis than any single questionnaire cut-off. You are building a case from several consistent measures, not leaning on one score.

Next step

Look at your last five overhead athletes. For each one, can you show a patient-nominated function score, a pain score that captures frequency, intensity and duration, and at least one objective measure as a percentage of expected, all recorded at consistent intervals? If not, that is the gap to close first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do standard shoulder questionnaires struggle in athletes?

Athletes start from a high baseline, so general questionnaires can reach their ceiling while the patient still cannot perform their sport. Symptoms may also appear only under high load, which a fixed list of daily tasks does not capture.

How does the Patient-Specific Functional Scale help with overhead athletes?

The patient names their own limiting activities, such as a serve or throw, and rates each from 0 to 10. This keeps the measure tied to the sport-specific goal and works best for tracking change within one patient over time.

Should a PROM alone decide return to sport?

No. Pair patient-reported function and the three-dimensional pain score with objective measures expressed as a percentage of expected score. When the two disagree, that mismatch is the most useful clinical finding.

Simple Strength Tests That Predict Long-Term Health, Not Just Recovery

Strength and Functional Capacity strength testing handheld dynamometry normative data sit-to-stand

Grip, sit-to-stand and handheld dynamometry can tell you more than whether a patient has recovered. How to measure strength against norms and act on it.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Why discharge strength is a health question, not only a rehab question

Most of us test strength to answer one question: has this patient recovered enough to stop? That is a legitimate use. It is also a narrow one. Muscle strength is one of the few things we measure that carries information beyond the presenting complaint, because it reflects how much physical reserve a person has to absorb the next injury, the next illness, or the next decade.

Large cohort studies outside this article's reference list have linked low grip strength and poor sit-to-stand performance with worse later-life outcomes, including disability and mortality. I have not cited a specific paper for that here because none in the approved evidence pack addresses it directly, so treat it as background you should verify in your own reading rather than a number to quote to patients. The clinical argument does not depend on a single hazard ratio. If a 58-year-old finishes treatment for a shoulder problem with a quadriceps or grip score well below what is expected for their age, sex and weight, the shoulder has not told you the whole story.

The practical point is that a rehab episode is often the only time a patient lets someone measure them. Using it to capture a baseline that is comparable and repeatable is cheap, and it changes what you can say at discharge.

What the rehabilitation evidence does and does not tell us

The trials we have are mostly about getting people better after a specific event. Reviews of physiotherapist-directed exercise after elective hip replacement have reported improvements in strength, gait speed and cadence (Coulter et al., 2013), and a randomised trial of a physiotherapy-led functional exercise programme after hip replacement looked at the same recovery window (Monaghan et al., 2017). A systematic review of early physiotherapy after total knee arthroplasty examined quality of life, pain and range of movement (Aftab et al., 2025).

These studies are useful for deciding whether to treat and how. They are less useful for answering the longer question: what strength level should this person be at in two years, and are they heading there? Follow-up in most rehabilitation trials is short. One trial in distal radius fracture followed patients for two years when comparing supervised physiotherapy with a home programme (Gutiérrez-Espinoza et al., 2024), which is unusual, and it is a reminder of how rarely we look that far out.

So the honest position is this. The rehab literature supports intervening on strength. It does not give you a discharge threshold that guarantees long-term health. What you can do is anchor each patient to a normative expectation and track the gap over time.

Three tests that earn their place in a 30-minute appointment

None of these needs more than a few minutes or any expensive hardware. All of them produce a number you can compare against a normative expectation rather than a grade out of five.

1. Handheld dynamometry for a lower-limb and a trunk-proximal muscle group

Handheld dynamometer data can be entered into Benchmark and compared against peer-reviewed normative values, expressed as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight. Choose one muscle group relevant to the complaint and one that is not. A knee extension score in a patient with a shoulder problem is not a distraction. It is the general-reserve measurement.

2. A maximum-repetition sit-to-stand or similar timed endurance task

Endurance in Benchmark means timed holds or maximum-repetition protocols. A repeated sit-to-stand from a standard chair height is quick, needs only a chair and a stopwatch, and reflects lower-limb capacity in a way patients recognise from daily life. Record repetitions in a fixed time, use the same chair height every visit, and compare against the expected score.

3. A gym-based lift for patients who already train

For patients who lift, a gym-based test such as a leg press or a goblet squat for repetitions can be compared with Strengthlevel normative data, again as a percentage of expectation. A fit 35-year-old who sits at 110% of expected has a different conversation ahead of them than one at 60%, even if both are pain-free.

Whichever you choose, standardise the position, the instruction, the number of trials and the rest between them. A measurement you cannot repeat next month is an opinion with a number attached.

Reading the result: percentage of expected, not pass or fail

Benchmark expresses strength as raw score against expected score, and as a percentage of normative expectation. It does not calculate a limb symmetry index or apply a fixed symmetry cut-off. That matters for this topic, because a patient can look symmetrical and still be weak across the board. If both sides are at 65% of expected, a left-to-right comparison would reassure you for no good reason. A comparison with norms would not.

A practical way to use the number:

  • At or above expected for age, sex and weight: record it, tell the patient it is their baseline, and re-test at follow-up.
  • Below expected: treat it as a finding in its own right, even if the presenting problem has settled. Benchmark suggests exercises when a patient falls below normative thresholds, and recommends sets, reps, load, rest, frequency and weekly volume per muscle group, with progressions and regressions.
  • Falling across repeated visits: that is worth more attention than a single low score, because it is a trajectory.

Pair the strength result with the Patient-Specific Functional Scale and the three-dimensional pain score. PSFS tells you whether the activities the patient cares about have changed. The strength percentage tells you whether capacity is moving with them. When PSFS improves and strength stays low, you have an early signal that the improvement may be fragile.

Making the strength gain last after discharge

A strength score at discharge is only as useful as the patient's habit of maintaining it. A panoramic review of interventions to improve adherence to physiotherapy in adults (Ley et al., 2024) is a useful starting point if you want to look at what the evidence says about techniques that help. The practical reading for a busy clinic is that adherence is something you design for rather than hope for.

Showing a patient their own number against an expected score gives them something concrete to hold. Telling someone they are at 72% of expected for their age and weight, and that the programme is aimed at moving that figure, is a different conversation from telling them to keep up the exercises.

Delivery mode is also worth thinking about. A randomised trial comparing digital with conventional physical therapy in chronic shoulder pain (Pak et al., 2023) and the ARTISAN trial of acute rehabilitation after traumatic anterior shoulder dislocation (Kearney et al., 2024) both ask, in different ways, how much supervised contact is needed. Neither was designed to settle how to keep strength up for years. A reasonable approach is to use contact time to set the target and the programme, then use a scheduled re-test to check it.

A simple routine you can start this week

  1. Pick one handheld dynamometry test and one repetition-based endurance test. Write down the position and instruction so every clinician in the clinic uses the same ones.
  2. Test every new patient over a set age, regardless of complaint, at the first or second appointment.
  3. Record the result as a percentage of expected for age, sex and weight.
  4. Re-test at discharge and again at a follow-up point you choose in advance.
  5. Where the result is below expected, build the gap into the discharge plan rather than leaving it as a footnote.

For clinic owners, the same routine has a clinical rationale before a commercial one. When every clinician measures in the same way, variation in what gets called good enough narrows, and the service becomes easier to explain to patients, referrers and commissioners.

Where to go from here

If you already test strength, the next step is to compare against norms and keep the data over time, rather than filing a one-off number in the notes. Benchmark PS is built for exactly that: technology-agnostic inputs, normative comparison, PSFS and pain scores alongside your objective results, and longitudinal tracking you can show a patient. Take your next five new patients, run the same two tests, and see what the percentages look like.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a simple strength test really say anything about long-term health?

Cohort research outside our reference pack links low grip and lower-limb strength with later disability and mortality, but it is observational. In clinic, treat a below-expected score as a flag for reduced physical reserve and a reason to plan ongoing strength work, not as a prediction for an individual.

What equipment do I need?

A handheld dynamometer for strength, and a standard chair and stopwatch for a repeated sit-to-stand. Benchmark is technology-agnostic, so you can enter data from the tools you already have and compare it as a percentage of expected score for age, sex and weight.

Does Benchmark compare left and right sides?

No. Benchmark does not calculate a limb symmetry index. It compares the patient's raw score with their expected score and expresses it as a percentage of normative expectation, which also catches patients who are weak on both sides.

How should I use the result at discharge?

Record it as a baseline, read it alongside the PSFS and three-dimensional pain score, and agree a re-test point with the patient. If the score is below expected, include a specific training plan in the discharge advice.

Continue Conservative Care or Refer On: The Decision Points in Lumbar Radiculopathy

Clinical Decision-Making lumbar radiculopathy referral decision-making handheld dynamometry PSFS

How to decide between continuing conservative care and referring on in lumbar radiculopathy, using red flags, objective strength data and PROM trajectory.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Most people with lumbar radiculopathy are managed in primary care physiotherapy, and most of them should be. The harder question is not whether to start conservative care. It is when to stop. Referring too early burdens spinal services and exposes patients to interventions they may not need. Referring too late leaves a patient with a progressing motor deficit or unrelenting pain in a treatment loop that is no longer helping.

Both errors have the same root. The decision to continue or escalate is often made on an impression of how the patient is doing, rather than on measurements that can be compared from one appointment to the next. This article sets out the decision points, and how objective data can sharpen each of them.

Decision point one: is this radiculopathy, and is anything urgent?

Before any question of continue or refer, you need to be confident about what you are managing. Leg pain with a lumbar origin is not always radiculopathy, and radiculopathy is not the only cause of leg symptoms in a back pain patient. Lumbar spinal stenosis presents with a different pattern, typically symptoms provoked by walking or standing and eased by flexion, and its management pathway differs in its own right (Lurie et al., 2016). Sacroiliac joint pain can also refer into the buttock and leg and is easily mistaken for a neural problem when the examination is thin (Migliorini et al., 2025).

Red flag screening sits above all of this. New bilateral leg symptoms, saddle sensory change, bladder or bowel disturbance, or rapidly progressive weakness are same-day escalation, not a trial of treatment. No outcome measure changes that. The role of measurement begins once those have been excluded.

  • Document the neurological examination in a way that can be repeated: myotomes, dermatomes and reflexes, recorded by the same method each visit.
  • Record which functional limitations brought the patient in, using the Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS), so that progress is judged against what matters to them.
  • Take a baseline three-dimensional pain score (the mean of frequency, intensity and duration), because a single numerical rating hides whether pain is becoming less frequent, shorter or just differently described.

Decision point two: is the neurological deficit stable, improving or worsening?

This is the decision that most clearly separates continue from refer. A stable or improving deficit under a coherent treatment plan supports carrying on. A deficit that is worsening, particularly progressive motor loss, changes the conversation regardless of how long the patient has been in treatment.

The weak point in routine practice is how motor deficit is recorded. Manual muscle grading is coarse. A patient can sit at grade 4 for weeks while their actual force output drifts up or down by a clinically meaningful amount. Handheld dynamometry gives a number that can be compared with age, sex and weight matched normative data from the peer-reviewed literature and expressed as a percentage of expected score. Ankle dorsiflexion, great toe extension, knee extension and hip abduction are practical candidates depending on the root level involved.

Two things matter in how you use it. First, trend matters more than any single value. A percentage of expected score that is rising across visits is reassuring even when it remains low. A score that falls is a prompt to re-examine and likely escalate. Second, Benchmark does not compare left with right, so the reference point is the normative expectation for the patient rather than the unaffected side. That is a better anchor when the contralateral limb is itself affected by deconditioning or pain avoidance.

Decision point three: is the treatment matched to what the data shows?

Before concluding that conservative care has failed, check that conservative care has actually been delivered. A patient who has been given a handful of generic exercises at low dose has not had an adequate trial. A decision to refer on is far more defensible when you can show what was prescribed: the load, sets, repetitions, frequency and progression, and what happened to the objective measures in response.

The evidence base for individual neural-targeted techniques is worth knowing. A systematic review of neural mobilisation in low back and radicular pain suggests it may help with pain and disability, but the authors work from a body of studies that varies in quality and technique, so it should be treated as one option within a broader plan rather than a stand-alone answer (Peacock et al., 2023). If you are using it, record the response in the same measures you use for everything else, so you can tell whether it is earning its place.

Where strength is below normative thresholds, Benchmark suggests exercises and recommends training parameters, including sets, reps, load, rest, frequency and weekly volume per muscle group and movement pattern, with progressions and regressions. The point for this decision is accountability. If dose has been appropriate and the objective picture is not moving, the case for escalation is much stronger than if the plan was vague.

Decision point four: is the trajectory behind what you would expect?

Time in treatment on its own is a poor trigger for referral, because patients start from different places and recover at different rates. A better question is whether this patient is changing at the rate that would be expected, and whether the change exceeds measurement noise.

PSFS and the three-dimensional pain score both have a minimal clinically important difference (MCID), and Benchmark presents these alongside an expected rate of change to aid interpretation. That gives you three practical readings at any review:

  1. Improving beyond MCID, objective measures rising. Continue, progress load, and plan discharge criteria around measurable function.
  2. Small change, within MCID, objective measures flat. Review the diagnosis and the dose. Change one variable and re-measure at the next visit.
  3. No change or deterioration despite adequate dose, or any worsening neurology. Refer on, with the data attached.

The middle category is where most uncertainty lives, and it is where subjective assessment tends to drift towards simply continuing. Having a stated threshold, agreed within the team, reduces the variation between clinicians seeing similar patients.

Decision point five: what makes a referral good?

A referral that says only that the patient has not responded to physiotherapy gives the receiving clinician very little. A referral supported by longitudinal data is easier to triage and harder to bounce. Include the baseline and current PSFS and three-dimensional pain scores against MCID, the neurological findings and how they have moved, handheld dynamometry as a percentage of expected score at each time point, the treatment dose delivered, and the patient's own account of what they cannot do.

This matters beyond convenience. Spinal services have to prioritise, and patients who arrive with clear documentation of progression or plateau are better placed in that process. It also protects the clinician: the rationale for escalating, or for continuing, is on the record and can be reviewed.

One caution on scope. The reference literature on conservative management in related conditions, such as lumbar stenosis, shows that decisions about surgical versus non-surgical care depend heavily on symptom severity, patient preference and trajectory (Lurie et al., 2016). Objective measures inform that discussion. They do not replace it, and the decision to operate belongs to the spinal team and the patient.

Making the decision consistent across your clinic

Variation in when clinicians refer is a clinical consistency problem before it is anything else. Two physiotherapists seeing the same patient on the same day may choose differently because each is weighing impressions rather than numbers. Agreeing which measures you collect, at what points, and what pattern triggers a review brings those decisions closer together. That consistency is also what makes a service easier to audit and defend, to commissioners and to patients alike.

It does not need expensive kit. A handheld dynamometer, an inclinometer for lumbar and hip range, a timed hold or repetition test for endurance, and two patient-reported measures cover a lot of ground. Where a patient's arm symptoms raise a neurogenic differential instead, such as thoracic outlet presentations, the same principle of structured, repeatable assessment applies (Dengler et al., 2022).

Next step

If you want to try this on your next few radiculopathy patients, start with three things: record a PSFS and three-dimensional pain score at baseline, take one dynamometry reading at the relevant myotome as a percentage of expected score, and agree in advance what a lack of change would mean. Benchmark PS holds these results longitudinally, shows them alongside MCID and expected rate of change, and gives you a record to hand to the next clinician if the answer is to refer on. Ask us for a walkthrough using one of your own cases.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should a physiotherapist refer on a patient with lumbar radiculopathy?

Immediately for red flags such as saddle sensory change, bladder or bowel disturbance, bilateral symptoms or rapidly progressive weakness. Otherwise, when there is worsening neurology, or no meaningful change in patient-reported and objective measures despite an adequately dosed programme.

How can you track motor deficit objectively in radiculopathy?

Handheld dynamometry at the relevant myotome, recorded as a percentage of expected score for the patient's age, sex and weight, and repeated at each review. The trend across visits is more informative than any single reading.

Which outcome measures does Benchmark PS use for radiculopathy?

The Patient-Specific Functional Scale and a three-dimensional pain score (frequency, intensity and duration), each interpreted alongside MCID and expected rate of change, together with strength, range of movement and endurance data.

Power Testing Without a Force Plate: What the Drop Jump Can Tell You in Clinic

Physical Performance Testing power testing drop jump reactive strength objective assessment

Drop jump testing gives you a reactive strength measure without a force plate. What it captures, how to run it with a smartphone, and how to act on the result.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Most testing batteries in physiotherapy stop at strength and range of movement. Power rarely gets tested at all, and when it does, it's usually because someone has access to a force plate in a university lab, not because it's practical in a 30-minute clinic slot. That gap matters more than it looks. An athlete can produce good peak force on a dynamometer and still fail to reproduce it quickly enough to be useful on a pitch, court, or track. The drop jump closes that gap, and you don't need a force plate to run it.

What a drop jump actually measures

The protocol is simple. The patient steps off a low box (20 to 30cm is standard for most clinical populations), lands, and immediately jumps for maximum height with minimum ground contact time. What you're capturing is reactive strength: the ability to absorb load and reapply force fast, rather than the ability to produce a single maximal effort. That's a different physical quality to the one strength testing measures, and it behaves differently across a rehab timeline.

You don't need a force plate or a contact mat to get a usable number from this. A smartphone app such as MyJump2 calculates flight time and ground contact time from slow-motion video, which is enough to derive jump height and a reactive strength index (jump height divided by contact time). Where an app isn't available, a simple vertical jump height measured against a wall or with a tape measure still gives you a repeatable power output figure, even without the timing component. The point is that the test is technology-agnostic: expensive equipment gets you more granularity, not a different test.

Why power deserves its own line in the notes, not just strength

The clearest evidence for testing power separately from strength comes from populations where clinicians have historically assumed strength recovery was the whole picture. In chronic ankle instability, strength and balance training protocols improve clinical outcome measures, but the outcome measures used to judge "recovered" status vary widely between studies, and functional performance measures often tell a different story to strength alone (Hall et al., 2018). A patient can regain comparable strength scores and still underperform on tasks that demand fast force production under load.

The same pattern shows up in return-to-sport decision-making after acute ankle sprain, where scoping reviews of the rehabilitation evidence highlight how inconsistently functional and performance-based criteria are applied compared with symptom resolution alone (Tee et al., 2022). If your discharge criteria are built entirely around strength and pain, you're missing a physical quality that behaves independently of both, and that quality happens to be one of the easiest to test with equipment you already own.

What this looks like in practice

  • A patient can hit an acceptable percentage of expected score on strength testing while still producing a flat, slow drop jump.
  • Power output often lags behind strength recovery, particularly in the weeks after strength testing first normalises.
  • Testing power separately gives you an earlier warning sign than waiting for a functional task to expose the deficit under sporting load.

Building the drop jump into a clinic session

A drop jump protocol fits into the same slot as your strength and range testing, provided you keep the setup consistent. A fixed box height, a fixed number of trials (three is usually enough once the patient understands the task), and a fixed instruction ("land and jump as high as possible, as fast as possible") remove most of the variability that would otherwise make session-to-session comparison meaningless.

The number that matters isn't the raw jump height or contact time in isolation. It's how that figure compares to the expected score for the patient's age, sex, and weight cohort. A 34-year-old five-a-side player and a 19-year-old academy footballer will have meaningfully different expected reactive strength values, and reporting a raw centimetre figure without that context tells you very little. Expressing the result as a percentage of normative expectation turns a single data point into something you can track and something you can explain to the patient in a way that means something to them.

When a patient sits below the expected threshold, the response isn't to push harder on the same test. It's to regress the loading demand: double-leg drop jumps before single-leg, lower box heights before higher ones, and pogo-style repeated jumps before reactive drop jumps, with sets, reps, and rest built around the deficit rather than a generic plyometric template. As the score climbs back towards expected range, the programme progresses in the same structured way.

What power-focused rehab evidence tells us about programming

Network meta-analyses comparing physiotherapy approaches for chronic ankle instability have found that programmes incorporating balance and functional training, rather than strength work in isolation, tend to deliver better treatment effects on patient-reported and functional outcomes (Yang et al., 2025). Hop-based and landing-focused training protocols specifically have been shown to improve landing mechanics and stability in athletes with chronic ankle instability compared with standard care (Ardakani et al., 2019), which supports building jump and landing tasks into rehab well before the final discharge session rather than treating them as a one-off clearance test.

This matters for the timing of rehab, not just the content. Accelerated rehabilitation protocols after acute ankle sprain that introduce functional loading earlier have produced faster return to function without compromising outcome (Bleakley et al., 2010), and early supervised physiotherapy following ankle sprain has shown similar benefit over unsupervised advice-based care (Brison et al., 2016). The pattern across this evidence is consistent: waiting for pain and swelling to fully settle before introducing any power-demand testing or training delays recovery unnecessarily. Drop jump testing, kept at a low box height and low volume early on, gives you a way to introduce that stimulus and monitor tolerance at the same time.

Where this applies beyond ankle rehab

The logic extends to any rehab pathway that ends in a return to running, jumping, or cutting sport, including tendon injuries where the final stage of rehab has to reintroduce fast, repeated loading before an athlete is cleared. Systematic reviews of early functional rehabilitation after Achilles tendon rupture note that structured, graded reintroduction of load, including plyometric-type demand, is a recurring feature of protocols associated with better outcomes, even though the specific timing and criteria used vary considerably between studies (Zellers et al., 2019). A drop jump test gives you an objective marker to decide when that reintroduction is appropriate, rather than relying on a fixed number of weeks post-injury.

In practice, a useful power testing pathway looks like this:

  1. Baseline drop jump (or simple vertical jump if timing equipment isn't available) once the patient is pain-free enough to land safely.
  2. Compare the result against normative expectation for age, sex, and weight, not against a generic benchmark borrowed from a different population.
  3. Where the score sits below expected range, prescribe specific loading progressions and regressions, with sets, reps, and frequency matched to the deficit.
  4. Retest at intervals through the rehab timeline, not just at the final session, so a plateau is visible before it becomes a discharge-day surprise.
  5. Track the trend alongside Patient-Specific Functional Scale scores and the three-dimensional pain score, so power output is read in context rather than in isolation.

Where to go from here

Power testing has been left out of most clinical batteries for a practical reason, not a clinical one: it looked like it needed a lab. It doesn't. A box, a stopwatch or a smartphone app, and a consistent protocol gives you a reactive strength measure that behaves differently to strength testing and catches deficits strength testing misses. Benchmark PS is built to make that measurement routine inside a normal appointment, whether you're using a smartphone app, a medicine ball, or a tape measure, comparing the result against normative expectation for the patient in front of you, and turning a below-threshold score into a specific loading plan rather than a vague note to "add some plyometrics."

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a force plate to test power in clinic?

No. A drop jump can be measured with a smartphone app such as MyJump2, which calculates jump height and ground contact time from video, or with a simple vertical jump measured against a wall or tape measure. A force plate adds precision but isn't required to get a usable, repeatable number.

What is reactive strength index and why does it matter?

Reactive strength index is jump height divided by ground contact time. It reflects how quickly a patient can absorb and reapply force, which is a different physical quality to peak strength and often lags behind strength recovery during rehab, making it a useful separate marker to track.

How often should drop jump power be retested during rehab?

Test at baseline once the patient can land safely without pain, then retest at intervals through the rehab timeline rather than only at final discharge. This makes a plateau or slow progression visible early, so the loading plan can be adjusted before it becomes a problem at the return-to-sport stage.

Outcome Data and Defensible Care: What Payers Actually Want to See

Defensible Care & Practice Economics outcome measurement defensible care PROMs commissioning

UK physio commissioners and insurers want standardised, comparable outcome data, not discharge narratives. Here's what actually holds up to scrutiny.

✓ Approve ✗ Skip ✎ Edit title

Every physiotherapist writes discharge summaries. Almost none of them are useful to the person paying for the episode of care. "Patient reports significant improvement, discharged with home exercise programme" tells a commissioner or insurer nothing they can act on. It is not comparable across patients, not comparable across clinicians, and not comparable across time. Payers of every kind, NHS integrated care boards, private medical insurers, corporate health schemes, are increasingly explicit that they want something else: measured, repeatable, standardised outcome data that lets them judge whether an episode of care actually worked.

This matters because the gap between what clinics record and what payers want is wider than most practice owners think, and it is a clinical measurement problem before it is a contracting problem.

What payers actually mean by "evidence of effectiveness"

Strip away the different funding mechanisms and NHS commissioners, insurers and corporate schemes are all asking a version of the same question: did this patient's function and symptoms change by a meaningful amount, measured the same way you'd measure it for the next patient. Insurers care about this because it lets them compare claims cost against outcome. Commissioners care because it lets them judge whether a conservative pathway is reducing pressure on surgical lists. Corporate schemes care because they're paying per episode and want to know the episode ended in something better than where it started.

The clinical literature that underpins reimbursement decisions is built on exactly this logic. Systematic reviews of physiotherapy after major procedures are constructed around a small set of comparable outcomes, pain, range of movement, quality of life, tracked at defined intervals so trials can be pooled and compared (Aftab et al., 2025). That is the standard payers have absorbed, whether or not they could name it. If your clinic cannot produce a baseline-to-discharge comparison on a fixed set of measures for a given condition, you are not speaking the same language as the person deciding whether to keep funding you.

The heterogeneity problem: why your data might not be comparable to anyone else's

It is tempting to assume the published evidence base at least gives everyone a shared yardstick. It doesn't, not reliably. A review of rehabilitation protocols following total knee arthroplasty found wide variation not just in what clinicians did with patients, but in which outcome measures they used to judge success in the first place (Dávila et al., 2019). A meta-analysis of rotator cuff repair rehabilitation protocols found the same fragmentation, different timelines, different progression criteria, different endpoints being called "success" (Bandara et al., 2021). Reviews of meniscus repair rehabilitation report the identical problem (Harput et al., 2020).

If specialist researchers publishing in peer-reviewed journals struggle to standardise what "outcome" means for a single procedure, the situation inside a typical MSK clinic is worse. One clinician charts range of movement in degrees, another writes "improved." One tracks a validated pain score, another writes "better than last week." Multiply that across a caseload and a payer trying to benchmark your clinic against itself over six months, let alone against a network average, has nothing solid to hold.

This is the practical argument for standardised testing, not an abstract one. A payer cannot act on data that changes shape from patient to patient. They can act on a discharge score that sits on the same scale as the baseline score, for every patient, every time.

What payers can actually act on

Three things travel well across a payer relationship, and none of them require expensive equipment or a research department.

  • The same patient-reported measure at baseline and discharge, for every patient. A Patient-Specific Functional Scale score plus a three-dimensional pain score (frequency, intensity, duration averaged) gives a payer two numbers they can track over time and compare across your caseload, rather than a paragraph of prose.
  • Objective performance data expressed against normative expectation, not raw numbers. "Quad strength improved" means nothing to a non-clinician. "Patient moved from 61% to 89% of expected strength for their age, sex and weight" is a number a commissioner can put in a report.
  • Evidence of adherence. Non-adherence is one of the largest hidden cost drivers in any funded pathway, and payers know it. Overviews of the intervention literature on physiotherapy adherence show how much outcome variance is attributable to whether patients actually did what was prescribed, not just what was prescribed (Ley et al., 2024). A clinic that can show session attendance and home programme compliance alongside outcome scores is answering a question the payer was already going to ask.

None of this requires a research-grade setup. A trial comparing digital, remotely-monitored physiotherapy against conventional in-person care for chronic shoulder pain found comparable clinical outcomes between the two delivery models, precisely because both arms were built around structured, session-level tracking rather than free-text notes (Pak et al., 2023). The lesson for a bricks-and-mortar clinic isn't that you need a digital delivery model, it's that structured tracking, done consistently, is what produces an auditable data trail in the first place.

Using outcome-driven decisions as your evidence base

The strongest cases clinics can make to payers are not just "our patients got better," they're "here is measured evidence that our pathway is at least as effective as the alternative, at lower cost and risk." The ACL SNNAP trial is a useful model of what that argument looks like when it's done properly: a large randomised comparison of surgical reconstruction against structured rehabilitation for non-acute ACL injury, built entirely around repeated, standardised outcome measurement rather than clinician impression (Beard et al., 2024). The result didn't just inform individual patient decisions, it gave commissioners a defensible basis for funding a conservative pathway as a legitimate first-line option rather than a fallback.

That is the template worth borrowing at clinic level, even without a trial infrastructure behind you. If you can show, with consistent measurement, that your conservative caseload achieves comparable functional outcomes to what the literature reports for surgical pathways in equivalent presentations, you have a genuine argument for reduced onward referral and a genuine case to bring to a payer conversation. Reviews of open kinetic chain exercise timing after ACL reconstruction exist precisely because the field keeps needing to answer, with data, whether a given approach changes outcomes enough to matter (Fontanier et al., 2025). Your clinic-level data should be answerable to the same standard, at a smaller scale.

Building a defensible outcome record in clinic

None of this needs to be complicated to build. It needs to be consistent.

  1. Use the same PROM for every patient with a given presentation, at baseline, at a defined mid-point, and at discharge.
  2. Score objective tests against normative expectation for age, sex and weight, not just as a raw number filed in the notes.
  3. Flag results against the minimal clinically important difference and expected rate of change, so "improved" means something statistically defensible rather than just directionally true.
  4. Track activity level against NHS guidelines and flag patients falling below recommended thresholds, a marker payers increasingly want to see addressed as part of holistic outcome, not just the presenting complaint.
  5. Capture the narrative record without burning appointment time on typing, structured clinical documentation generated from a session recording keeps the qualitative detail intact while the quantitative side does the heavy lifting for payer conversations.
  6. Visualise PROM and objective performance data together, longitudinally, so the artefact you hand a commissioner or insurer is a chart, not a summary paragraph.

The clinical case comes first. Standardised measurement exists because it produces better decisions for the patient in front of you, tighter progression criteria, clearer discharge thresholds, less guesswork. The commercial case follows from that, not the other way round. A clinic that measures consistently ends up, almost as a byproduct, holding exactly the kind of data payers have been asking for all along.

Where to start

If your current outcome record is a mix of free-text notes and inconsistent measures, the fix isn't a new reporting template, it's consistent capture at the point of care. Frequently Asked Questions

What outcome data do payers actually expect from a physiotherapy clinic?

Consistent baseline-to-discharge scores on the same patient-reported measure for every patient, objective performance data expressed against normative expectation rather than raw numbers, and some evidence of treatment adherence. A discharge narrative alone is not enough.

Why isn't a discharge letter sufficient evidence for insurers or commissioners?

Because it isn't comparable across patients, clinicians, or time. Payers need standardised numbers they can track and benchmark, not prose that varies from note to note.

Does building a defensible outcome record require expensive equipment or software?

No. Standardised patient-reported measures and objective tests scored against normative data can be captured with low-tech tools. What matters is consistency, using the same measures the same way for every patient, not the sophistication of the equipment.
