Most shoulder questionnaires were never validated on athletes.

Self-reported tools dominate shoulder outcome tracking because they are quick and cheap to administer. A systematic review of self-reported measures for rotator cuff disorders found responsiveness and minimal clinically important difference data missing or poorly reported for many in routine use (St-Pierre et al., 2016). If you cannot say what change on a scale represents meaningful improvement, you cannot use it to justify a discharge decision.

Condition-specific scales are built around activities of daily living, not the demands of throwing, serving or overhead lifting. A score that asks about combing your hair tells you little about whether a bowler can tolerate a full training load.

The physical examination feeding into these assessments has its own reliability problems. A review of shoulder special tests found inconsistent inter-rater reliability across studies and settings (Lange et al., 2017), so any outcome measure built on top inherits that uncertainty.

For overhead athletes, pair a patient-specific measure such as the Patient-Specific Functional Scale with objective strength testing, rather than relying on a generic questionnaire alone.
